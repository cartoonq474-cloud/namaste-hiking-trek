Annapurna Circuit Trek overview
The Annapurna Circuit Trek is one of Nepal's most geographically diverse long-distance trekking journeys. Instead of following one valley toward a single mountain objective and returning along the same route, the circuit travels around the Annapurna massif, linking the Marsyangdi Valley in the east with the Kali Gandaki Valley in the west. Along the way, trekkers move through subtropical foothills, terraced farmland, pine and rhododendron forests, high-altitude settlements, alpine terrain, the dry landscape of the Manang and Mustang regions, historic trading villages, major pilgrimage sites and some of the most recognizable mountain viewpoints in the Nepal Himalaya.

The classic route crosses Thorong La Pass at 5,416 m (17,769 ft), making the pass the highest point of the standard circuit. Nepal Tourism Board describes the Annapurna Circuit as a major trekking route that progresses from subtropical conditions toward the trans-Himalayan environment of Manang and Mustang, crosses Thorong La and follows the Kali Gandaki corridor.

The exact form of the circuit has changed over time. Historically, trekkers walked much farther through the lower Marsyangdi Valley, beginning around Besisahar and spending several days on foot before reaching the higher villages. Road construction has since reached many settlements along the lower circuit and the Kali Gandaki side, so modern itineraries frequently combine walking with jeep transportation. Nepal Tourism Board still presents Besisahar as the traditional gateway, while its current information also recognizes road and transport access within the Annapurna region.

That development has changed the route without eliminating its central trekking experience. The high route through Pisang and Manang, the ascent toward Thorong La, the descent to Muktinath, the historic settlements of Kagbeni and Marpha, the Kali Gandaki landscape, the forests around Ghorepani and the Poon Hill viewpoint remain central components of the journey.

For many trekkers, the defining feature of the Annapurna Circuit is not simply the altitude. It is the extraordinary sequence of change. You can begin the journey in a warm river valley surrounded by crops and dense vegetation, spend several days climbing through increasingly cooler forests, reach the dry high-altitude world of Manang, cross a 5,416-meter pass and then descend into the trans-Himalayan landscape of Mustang. Later, the route drops into warmer conditions again before climbing through rhododendron forest toward Ghorepani.

That degree of environmental variation within a single trek gives the Annapurna Circuit a character that is difficult to reproduce elsewhere.
What is the Annapurna Circuit Trek?
The Annapurna Circuit is a trekking route that circumnavigates the Annapurna massif and crosses the high watershed at Thorong La before descending through the Kali Gandaki Valley.

The route is generally traveled in a counterclockwise direction. A traditional sequence begins around Besisahar, follows the Marsyangdi River upstream, reaches Chame, continues toward Pisang and Manang, climbs through Yak Kharka toward Thorong Phedi, crosses Thorong La and then descends through Muktinath, Kagbeni and Jomsom. The route can then continue through Marpha, Tukuche, Ghasa and Tatopani before climbing toward Ghorepani and Poon Hill, eventually reaching the Pokhara area.

This direction matters because the circuit's highest terrain is approached progressively rather than immediately. Manang becomes the key acclimatization point before the trek moves above 4,000 m.

The route also creates a genuine sense of geographical progression. You are not simply walking toward a mountain. You are moving around an entire mountain system and repeatedly entering new valleys, settlements and ecological zones.

The circuit is therefore best understood through several connected experiences:

The Marsyangdi Valley provides the approach through the central hills and increasingly high mountain terrain.

Manang marks the transition into the high-altitude, Tibetan-influenced northern section.

Thorong La provides the highest point and the principal physical objective.

Muktinath introduces one of the Himalaya's most important Hindu and Buddhist pilgrimage landscapes.

Kagbeni and Jomsom connect the trek to the historic Kali Gandaki trade corridor and the Mustang region.

Marpha and the Thakali settlements introduce a distinctive mountain culture strongly associated with trade, hospitality and local food traditions.

Tatopani returns the journey to a much warmer environment and provides access to natural hot springs.

Ghorepani and Poon Hill finish the trek with dense forest and broad mountain panoramas.

The result is more than a sequence of overnight stops. Each section changes what the trek feels like.
Where is the Annapurna Circuit located?
The Annapurna Circuit is located in central Nepal within the wider Annapurna region and the Annapurna Conservation Area. The region covers a large range of elevations and landscapes, stretching from lower hill country toward high Himalayan and trans-Himalayan terrain.

Nepal Tourism Board describes the Annapurna region as one of Nepal's major trekking destinations, with routes ranging from short journeys to full circuits lasting several weeks. It identifies the Annapurna massif, Dhaulagiri, the Kali Gandaki gorge, Manang, Mustang, Muktinath and Tilicho Lake among the region's principal natural and cultural features.

The route's location explains much of its environmental diversity.

The southern and lower valleys receive considerably more moisture and support dense vegetation and agriculture. As the trail climbs northward, the landscape becomes progressively drier. Manang lies within a rain-shadow environment, producing a more arid appearance despite its proximity to very high mountains. Nepal Tourism Board specifically notes the stark, rocky character of the Manang landscape and its similarity to the Tibetan plateau.

After crossing Thorong La, the route reaches another distinctly dry environment around Muktinath, Kagbeni and Jomsom.

Farther south, the Kali Gandaki Valley becomes progressively greener, eventually reaching the warm forests around Tatopani and Ghorepani.
The Annapurna Circuit is defined by elevation change
Altitude is one of the most important physical characteristics of the Annapurna Circuit.

The lower traditional starting area around Besisahar lies at roughly 760 m, while Thorong La reaches 5,416 m. Modern itineraries often begin the main walking section higher because of road access, but the route still involves a major elevation transition.

Sleeping-altitude progression is especially important. The sequence below also includes the daytime Thorong La crossing; the pass is not an overnight stop.

A typical modern sequence can move through:
- Chame at approximately 2,710 m.
- Pisang at approximately 3,200 m.
- Manang at approximately 3,500 m.
- Yak Kharka at approximately 4,000 m.
- Thorong Phedi at approximately 4,450 m.
- Thorong La at 5,416 m.
- Muktinath at approximately 3,800 m.
The exact overnight altitude varies by itinerary and accommodation location.

The important point is not the numerical value of every stop by itself. It is the rate at which sleeping altitude increases.

The CDC's current altitude-travel guidance emphasizes gradual ascent and acclimatization because susceptibility to altitude illness cannot be predicted simply from physical fitness. At higher elevations, lower atmospheric pressure means less oxygen is available with each breath, and the body requires time to adapt.

For this reason, a well-structured Annapurna Circuit itinerary normally includes a dedicated acclimatization period in Manang before the highest section.
Thorong La is the high point, not the entire trek
Thorong La receives much of the attention because it is the highest point of the circuit.

At 5,416 m, the pass represents the physical midpoint of the journey in more than one sense. Before the pass, the trek is primarily an ascent through the Marsyangdi and Manang valleys. After the pass, the journey becomes a long descent through Muktinath, Mustang and the Kali Gandaki.

The crossing is demanding because it combines altitude with duration.

A typical crossing begins early in the morning from Thorong Phedi or High Camp. The route climbs steeply through thin air and may involve snow, ice or strong wind depending on weather and season. After reaching the summit, the descent toward Muktinath remains physically demanding because the trail loses more than 1,500 vertical meters.

A trekker may therefore spend many hours both climbing and descending on the same day.

The summit is important, but the route immediately afterward is equally significant. Reaching Muktinath after the crossing marks the beginning of a completely different environmental and cultural section.
The Annapurna Circuit connects the Marsyangdi and Kali Gandaki valleys
The geographical structure of the circuit is one of its greatest strengths.

The Marsyangdi side is a river valley trek.

The route follows the river upward through villages, forests and increasingly large mountain walls.

The environment becomes drier and more exposed as it approaches Manang.

The journey then crosses the high divide at Thorong La.

Afterward, the route descends through the Muktinath and Kali Gandaki landscapes.

This means the circuit connects two major Himalayan river systems through one continuous trekking route.

The transformation is obvious on foot.

The lower Marsyangdi contains extensive vegetation and agricultural land.

Around Manang, the landscape becomes rocky and high-altitude.

Near Thorong Phedi, trees largely disappear.

Beyond the pass, the landscape is dry and open.

Around Tatopani, vegetation becomes dense again.

Near Ghorepani, the landscape is once again dominated by forest.

The circuit is therefore not simply a mountain route. It is a journey through contrasting environmental systems.
What the Annapurna Circuit Trek is really like
Descriptions of the Annapurna Circuit can make it sound like a sequence of spectacular photographs connected by comfortable walking days. The reality is more varied.

The route is physically demanding.

It contains long walking days, steep uphill climbs, substantial descents, cold weather, exposure to altitude and changing trail conditions.

At the same time, many sections are perfectly manageable for trekkers who prepare correctly.

The biggest mistake is to judge the trek entirely by the distance walked.

Altitude changes how the body experiences effort.

A five-hour walking day at moderate elevation can feel completely different from a five-hour day around 4,000 m.

Similarly, a short downhill section after Thorong La can be surprisingly hard because your legs are already tired from the ascent and summit crossing.

The trek also has a psychological rhythm.

Some days feel long.

Some feel unexpectedly easy.

Some are dominated by mountain scenery.

Others are more interesting because of the people, villages and cultural details along the route.

The most memorable experience often comes from the contrast between those days.
Expect around five to seven hours of walking on many days
A common trekking day begins around 6:00 or 7:00 in the morning.

You wake up, have breakfast, pack your day bag and begin walking with your guide and group.

The main luggage is generally carried separately by porters or transported as part of the trekking operation.

Walking then continues through a combination of steady ascents, descents, level traverses and village sections.

Lunch is usually taken at a teahouse or another local establishment along the route.

The afternoon continues until the next overnight settlement.

Daily walking time depends on route, altitude, pace, road conditions and the specific itinerary. A responsible operator should avoid presenting the schedule as a guaranteed number of hours because mountain travel is affected by factors beyond the operator's control.

The Thorong La day is the exception.

It begins much earlier.

The route is longer and the combination of altitude and cold makes the walking substantially more demanding.

Near the lower Marsyangdi, you encounter roads, bridges, terraced fields, forest and river landscapes.

Around Chame, pine forests become more prominent and the mountains appear closer.

The approach toward Pisang combines steep walls, open valley views and increasingly dry terrain.

The Annapurna Circuit is not a technical mountaineering expedition
The standard Annapurna Circuit is a trekking route, not a technical climbing expedition.

Most seasonal crossings do not require ropes, ice axes or advanced mountaineering skills.

The trail is established and supported by teahouses and local communities.

However, this does not mean the trek should be treated casually.

At 5,416 m, Thorong La is a serious high-altitude environment.

Snow and ice can affect the trail.

Strong winds can make the pass more exposed.

Altitude illness remains possible.

The descent can be steep and physically demanding.

The difference between "non-technical" and "easy" is therefore important.

You do not normally need technical climbing skills.

You do need endurance, preparation, appropriate equipment, conservative pacing and respect for altitude and weather.
The road has changed the Annapurna Circuit
Road construction is now a permanent part of the modern Annapurna trekking experience.

Jeep roads reach many lower and western settlements that were historically accessible primarily by foot.

This has shortened several walking sections and created transportation alternatives.

For trekkers with limited time, this can be useful.

A jeep ride from Besisahar toward Chame can eliminate several days of lower-valley walking.

On the western side, vehicle travel around the Kali Gandaki can shorten the distance between Jomsom, Marpha, Tatopani and other settlements.

But the road also changes the character of some sections.

The exact proportion of walking and driving should therefore be stated clearly when a package is advertised as an Annapurna Circuit Trek.

A "14-day Annapurna Circuit" can mean something very different from a traditional three-week itinerary if several sections are traveled by road.

For a modern itinerary, the important question is not whether roads exist. It is which sections are intentionally walked and which are covered by vehicle.
The high route through Pisang is a major walking experience
Between Pisang and Manang, trekkers commonly encounter lower and higher route options.

Ghyaru and Ngawal also demonstrate how settlement patterns adapt to elevation and climate.

Manang marks the transition to high-altitude trekking
Manang is more than a convenient overnight settlement.

It is the point at which the circuit's environment becomes distinctly high altitude and the trekking strategy changes.

The broad valley, dry slopes and Tibetan-influenced architecture create an atmosphere unlike the lower Marsyangdi settlements.

Manang is also an essential acclimatization location on many classic itineraries.

The principle is simple.

You spend time at around 3,500 m rather than immediately moving toward the 4,000-meter sleeping elevations above.

A daytime hike can take you to a higher point before returning to Manang to sleep.

The Thorong La crossing is a commitment day

You wake before sunrise.

The first section is climbed in darkness.

The pace is deliberately controlled.

Once the pass is crossed, the route continues down toward Muktinath.

The hardest work is therefore concentrated into a single extended day.

This is why preparation in the preceding days matters so much.

You need enough physical reserve to continue after reaching the summit.

Muktinath
Muktinath sits at approximately 3,800 m and is one of Nepal's most important pilgrimage sites.

Tilicho Lake, alternative high routes and additional mountain viewpoints can extend the experience.

However, experience at lower elevations does not automatically remove altitude risk.

Each trekker still needs to acclimatize.
For families
Families can consider selected sections of the Annapurna region, but a complete circuit requires careful planning.

Younger children may struggle with the duration of the walking days and the altitude above Manang.

Older children with hiking experience may be able to manage the route with appropriate supervision.

The Thorong La day is the most demanding section.

Family itineraries should therefore be designed around conservative pacing and flexibility rather than maximum daily distance.
For older trekkers
Age itself does not determine whether someone can trek the Annapurna Circuit.

The more important considerations are physical preparation, experience with long-distance walking, response to altitude and the ability to maintain a sensible pace.

Older trekkers may benefit from porter support and a conservative itinerary.

Any traveler with a relevant medical concern should obtain individualized medical advice before undertaking a trek reaching 5,416 m.
For solo travelers
Solo travelers can participate in the circuit within guided arrangements.

Nepal Tourism Board's current TIMS framework lists the Annapurna Circuit among the routes where trekkers are required to be accompanied by a licensed trekking guide and carry an agency-issued TIMS card.

That means the modern solo-traveler experience is different from an independent unsupported solo trek.

Traveling alone as a customer does not necessarily mean trekking without support.

A licensed guide can provide navigation, cultural interpretation, logistics and assistance if conditions become difficult.
The importance of physical preparation
The best preparation is not necessarily high-intensity gym training.

The circuit rewards walking endurance.

Regular long walks.

Hill hikes.

Stair climbing.

Incline treadmill training.

Back-to-back weekend hikes.

Strength exercises for the legs.

Training with the daypack and footwear you intend to use.

These activities prepare the body for the repetitive nature of the route.

The objective is not to become an elite athlete.

The objective is to make several hours of walking feel familiar before arriving in Nepal.
Altitude requires a different kind of preparation
Physical fitness improves your ability to walk.

It does not guarantee acclimatization.

The body must still adapt to altitude.

This is why the Manang acclimatization day is a core component of a responsible itinerary.

The CDC's altitude guidance emphasizes that individual susceptibility varies and that travelers should ascend gradually and respond appropriately when symptoms develop.

A trekker who feels unexpectedly strong at 3,000 m should not interpret that as proof that altitude will never become a problem at 4,500 m.

Altitude should be managed continuously.
Accommodation expectations
Accommodation is primarily provided through teahouses and trekking lodges.

The standard experience ranges from relatively comfortable rooms in developed villages to very basic accommodation in remote high-altitude areas.

At lower and middle elevations, private rooms may be widely available.

As altitude and demand increase, shared rooms become more common.

Dining rooms are often the warmest communal spaces.

Sleeping rooms may be cold.

Hot showers and Wi-Fi can be available in many places but should not be treated as guaranteed services throughout the route.

The correct mindset is to appreciate the variability rather than expect hotel-style consistency.
- Food Expectations
Food is generally available throughout the route.

Dal bhat is one of the most practical meals because it is filling and widely prepared.

Other common choices include noodles, fried rice, momos, pasta, soups, potatoes, pancakes and eggs.

The food experience becomes especially distinctive around the Kali Gandaki, where Thakali culinary traditions and local apple products become part of the journey.

At higher elevations, supply limitations reduce variety.

Energy and warmth become more important than menu sophistication.
- Road Expectations
The modern Annapurna Circuit includes roads.

Travelers who expect an entirely trail-only journey from Besisahar to Pokhara may be surprised by the current infrastructure.

A professionally designed package should clearly identify where transport is used.

Road access can be a logistical advantage because it enables flexible starts, shorter itineraries and easier emergency exits.

At the same time, some trekkers deliberately choose longer walking itineraries to preserve more of the traditional trail experience.

Neither approach changes the fundamental nature of the high route.

Thorong La remains a walking crossing.

The Manang section remains a trekking environment.

The mountain and cultural landscapes remain the core attraction.
Weather and flexibility
No Annapurna Circuit itinerary should be presented as completely rigid.

Weather can affect roads.

Snow can affect high passes.

Rain can affect lower valleys.

Wind can affect the Kali Gandaki.

Transport can experience delays.

Accommodation capacity can fluctuate during busy periods.

Individual trekkers can also require changes because of altitude response, fatigue or minor injuries.

The best itinerary is therefore one that has enough flexibility to adjust without compromising the entire journey.
Who will enjoy the Annapurna Circuit most?
The route is particularly suited to travelers who value variety.

People who want only one famous mountain viewpoint may prefer a shorter trek.

The Annapurna Circuit rewards travelers who enjoy the journey between the major highlights.

It rewards people who are interested in villages as much as peaks.

It rewards travelers who enjoy seeing ecological transitions.

It rewards those who appreciate local food, religious architecture, historic trade routes and mountain communities.

It rewards trekkers who are willing to walk slowly when altitude demands it.

The Annapurna Circuit's enduring appeal comes from the fact that it combines several kinds of travel into one route.

It is a high-altitude trek.

It is a cultural journey.

It is an ecological traverse.

It is a pilgrimage landscape.

It is a village-to-village walking experience.

You begin among warm valleys and agricultural settlements.

You move into forests and increasingly dramatic mountain landscapes.

You reach Manang and acclimatize in a high-altitude cultural environment.

You climb toward Thorong La.

That sequence is the real Annapurna Circuit.

The circuit remains a journey around a mountain range rather than merely a route to a mountain viewpoint.

For a trekker prepared for sustained walking, high-altitude exposure, changing weather and basic mountain accommodation, it provides an unusually complete experience of the Nepal Himalaya.

The route demands respect at its highest point.

It also rewards patience throughout every section before and after the pass.

A well-designed Annapurna Circuit itinerary therefore does not focus only on reaching Thorong La.

It balances the entire journey: gradual acclimatization, sensible walking days, realistic transport arrangements, adequate rest, cultural awareness, environmental responsibility and enough flexibility to respond to actual mountain conditions.

The result is a trek that combines altitude with diversity rather than altitude alone.

That is the central idea behind the Annapurna Circuit.

It is not simply a climb to 5,416 m.

It is the experience of traveling around the Annapurna massif through changing ecosystems, communities and landscapes, crossing one of the world's great Himalayan passes and continuing into an entirely different valley before finally returning to the lower mountains.

For many trekkers, that completeness is what defines the Annapurna Circuit.
Annapurna Circuit Trek highlights
The Annapurna Circuit Trek brings together an extraordinary collection of mountain, cultural and natural highlights within one continuous Himalayan journey. The route is famous for Thorong La Pass, but the pass is only one part of a much larger experience that includes the high-altitude village of Manang, the sacred pilgrimage site of Muktinath, the historic settlement of Kagbeni, the Thakali villages of the Kali Gandaki, the apple orchards of Marpha, the hot springs of Tatopani, the rhododendron forests around Ghorepani and the sunrise panorama from Poon Hill.

What makes these highlights particularly memorable is the way they appear in sequence.

The landscape does not remain the same for more than a few days. You move from warm river valleys into forests, from forests into high mountain terrain, from the high plateau of Manang into the dry Mustang landscape, and from the arid Kali Gandaki corridor back into lush forest before reaching Pokhara.

The cultural experience changes in the same way.

Lower sections introduce the hill communities and agricultural traditions of central Nepal. The Manang Valley brings a stronger Tibetan Buddhist influence and a long history of trans-Himalayan trade. Muktinath brings together Hindu and Buddhist pilgrimage traditions. The Kali Gandaki introduces Thakali culture, historic trade settlements and local food traditions.

The route therefore offers far more than a list of viewpoints.

Each major highlight explains something about the mountains, the people who live there and the way the trail has developed over generations.
Cross Thorong La pass at 5,416 m
Crossing Thorong La Pass at 5,416 m (17,769 ft) is the defining physical achievement of the Annapurna Circuit.

The pass is the highest point on the standard route and the moment toward which the entire upper itinerary gradually builds. By the time you reach Thorong Phedi or High Camp, you have already spent several days ascending through the Marsyangdi and Manang valleys. Your body has been exposed to increasing altitude, your walking pace has slowed naturally, and the surrounding landscape has become increasingly barren.

Then comes the pass day.

The crossing usually begins before sunrise. From Thorong Phedi, the route climbs steadily toward the pass, often in cold darkness during the first part of the morning. Headlamps illuminate the trail while the surrounding mountains remain silhouettes. As daylight develops, the landscape becomes more visible and the scale of the surrounding terrain becomes easier to appreciate.

The physical challenge is substantial because of the altitude.

At 5,416 m, the air contains considerably less oxygen than at sea level. Even well-conditioned trekkers move slowly. The climb is not difficult because of technical climbing requirements. It is difficult because every step takes place in thin air after several days of accumulated exertion.

The final approach to the pass can feel psychologically demanding because the summit may appear close while still requiring significant effort to reach.

When you arrive, prayer flags and the summit marker provide a clear sense of completion.

On clear days, the surrounding panorama includes major peaks and ridges of the Annapurna and Dhaulagiri regions. The visual experience is made more dramatic by the fact that you have reached the summit on foot.

But reaching Thorong La is not the end of the day.

The trail immediately begins descending toward Muktinath. The route loses more than 1,500 vertical meters from the pass to the settlement, meaning the descent can be as physically demanding as the climb in a different way.

Your quadriceps, knees and calves have already worked hard during the approach. The long descent then requires careful footing on a steep, sometimes loose trail.

This combination makes Thorong La a true endurance highlight rather than a simple summit photo.
Why Thorong La is so significant
Thorong La is important for three connected reasons.

First, it is the highest point of the classic circuit.

Second, it represents the geographical transition between the Marsyangdi side and the Kali Gandaki side.

Third, it is the point where the accumulated effort of the preceding days reaches its maximum physical expression.

Unlike a short summit excursion from a nearby lodge, Thorong La requires a multi-day approach.

The pass is earned through the entire route.

That is one reason trekkers frequently describe the crossing as the moment that defines the circuit.
The best way to experience the pass
The strongest pass experience comes from treating the crossing as a mountain objective rather than a race.

Start early.

Maintain a slow, deliberate pace.

Follow your guide's instructions.

Keep warm layers accessible.

Carry sufficient water and food.

Monitor how your body responds.

Do not assume that reaching High Camp means the crossing is guaranteed.

Weather and altitude conditions can change quickly.

The pass deserves respect because the combination of elevation, wind and cold creates an environment that is fundamentally different from ordinary trekking terrain.
Visit the sacred temple of Muktinath
After Thorong La, the route descends toward Muktinath, a major pilgrimage destination situated at approximately 3,800 m.

Muktinath is one of the most culturally significant stops on the Annapurna Circuit because it is sacred to both Hindus and Tibetan Buddhists.

For Hindu pilgrims, Muktinath is associated with Lord Vishnu and forms part of an important pilgrimage tradition. For Buddhist pilgrims, the site is associated with Buddhist religious traditions and Guru Rinpoche.

This shared sacred landscape is one of the features that makes Muktinath unusual within a Himalayan trekking route.
- The Religious Complex
The Muktinath complex contains several important features.

The temple area is associated with Vishnu worship.

A set of 108 water spouts forms one of the best-known features of the pilgrimage site.

The Jwala Mai shrine is associated with the natural flame that has made the site especially significant.

A Buddhist gompa stands within the wider religious landscape.

These features exist within a mountain environment that is already visually dramatic.

The surrounding landscape is relatively dry compared with the southern Annapurna slopes.

Brown and ochre hills rise around the valley.

Snow peaks appear beyond the nearer ridges.

The contrast between the austere Mustang landscape and the religious activity at the site makes Muktinath especially memorable.
Why trekkers should spend time at Muktinath
Muktinath should not be treated merely as the place where you sleep after crossing Thorong La.

The site provides a rare opportunity to observe a living Himalayan pilgrimage tradition.

During trekking and pilgrimage seasons, visitors arrive from Nepal and India, while Buddhist pilgrims and local residents also move through the complex.

This creates an atmosphere very different from a mountain viewpoint.

The appropriate experience is slower and more observant.

Respect religious boundaries.

Dress appropriately.

Ask before photographing people or religious ceremonies.

Avoid treating active religious spaces as photo props.

The value of Muktinath lies precisely in the fact that it remains a functioning spiritual center.
Walk the Kali Gandaki valley
The Kali Gandaki Valley is one of the defining geographical features of the Annapurna Circuit.

After crossing Thorong La and moving through Muktinath and Kagbeni, the route follows the broad Kali Gandaki corridor toward Jomsom and farther south.

The valley is enclosed by some of the highest mountains in the world, including Dhaulagiri to the west and Annapurna I to the east.

The landscape is dramatically different from the Marsyangdi side.

The upper Kali Gandaki is dry and exposed.

Mountain slopes are often brown, gray or reddish rather than densely forested.

Wind moves through the valley, particularly during the afternoon.

Agriculture depends heavily on irrigation.

Stone settlements appear adapted to the dry climate.
The historic importance of the valley
The Kali Gandaki is not merely a beautiful river valley.

It has long been a major corridor for trade and movement between the Tibetan plateau and the lower Himalayan regions.

Communities developed around this geography.

Villages became trading centers.

Hospitality became economically important.

The movement of salt and grain created a regional commercial network.

Walking through the valley therefore means traveling through a landscape shaped by centuries of human movement.
The geological scale of the Kali Gandaki
The valley is particularly impressive because of the scale difference between the river corridor and the surrounding mountains.

The terrain rises dramatically from the valley floor toward peaks that reach more than 8,000 m.

This creates the sense of walking through an enormous vertical landscape.

The view is not simply a line of mountains in the distance.

The mountains rise directly from the surrounding terrain.

Cliffs, ridges and side valleys create constant variation.

This is one reason the Kali Gandaki remains one of the most memorable sections even after the major physical objective of Thorong La has been completed.
Watch sunrise from Poon Hill
Poon Hill, at approximately 3,210 m, is one of Nepal's most celebrated mountain viewpoints and a memorable final highlight of the longer Annapurna Circuit.

The viewpoint is reached from Ghorepani, usually before sunrise.

The early morning experience is part of the attraction.

You leave the teahouse while it is still dark.

The temperature can be cold.

A headlamp is usually necessary.

The trail climbs steadily toward the viewpoint.

As more trekkers arrive, the hilltop gradually fills with people waiting for the first light.

Then the horizon begins to change.

The surrounding mountains emerge from darkness.

Dhaulagiri appears prominently to the west.

The Annapurna range rises to the north.

Annapurna South, Hiunchuli and Machhapuchhare become increasingly visible as the sky brightens.

The first sunlight reaches the higher peaks.

The color of the mountains changes from gray to gold before becoming brighter as the sun rises.
Why Poon Hill is different from Thorong La
Thorong La and Poon Hill provide completely different trekking experiences.

Thorong La is about effort, altitude and the achievement of crossing a major high mountain pass.

Poon Hill is primarily about perspective.

You are not crossing a dangerous high pass.

You are climbing to a viewpoint where a large section of the Himalayan skyline can be observed at once.

The emotional effect is different.

After nearly two weeks of trekking, Poon Hill becomes a final panoramic summary of the journey.

The warmth of the lower altitude also changes the experience.

You are no longer sleeping above 4,000 m.

You are moving through forest rather than barren alpine terrain.

The final sunrise therefore feels like both a viewpoint and a conclusion.
Experience the acclimatization village of Manang
Manang, at approximately 3,500 m, is one of the most important destinations on the Annapurna Circuit.

It serves as the principal acclimatization point for many itineraries, but its significance extends well beyond altitude management.

The village is situated in a broad, dry valley surrounded by high Himalayan peaks.

Its architecture reflects centuries of adaptation to a cold and relatively dry mountain climate.

Stone buildings and compact settlement patterns are common.

Buddhist religious structures are prominent.

The village contains teahouses, shops, cafés, small services and facilities that support trekkers and local residents.
Manang as an acclimatization base
The principal reason for staying in Manang is altitude.

Moving directly from the lower valley toward Thorong La without an acclimatization period increases the physical demands of the upper route.

The standard acclimatization strategy is to remain in Manang for an additional night while making a daytime hike to higher elevation.

Possible objectives include higher ridges, Gangapurna Lake or the Ice Lake area, depending on the itinerary, weather and physical condition of the group.

The goal is not simply to collect another viewpoint.

The purpose is to expose the body to higher elevation while returning to Manang to sleep.
Manang as a cultural highlight
The village itself deserves exploration.

You can observe traditional architecture, visit Buddhist religious sites and walk through the settlement's narrow lanes.

The surrounding valley also demonstrates how agriculture has adapted to high elevation and limited moisture.

Manang therefore functions simultaneously as a logistical center, cultural settlement and physiological preparation point.

That combination makes it one of the most important highlights of the circuit.
Walk through rhododendron forests to Ghorepani
The route from Tatopani toward Ghorepani provides one of the most dramatic ecological transitions of the entire Annapurna Circuit.

After days spent in dry high-altitude landscapes, the trail becomes green and forested.

The route climbs through hillsides dominated by rhododendron and other temperate forest species.

The forest can feel surprisingly cool even after the warmer conditions around Tatopani.

In spring, flowering rhododendrons add another dimension to the landscape.

The blossoms can cover large sections of the hillside and create a striking contrast against the green forest and distant snow peaks.
The ecological importance of the forest
The forest is not simply scenery.

It forms part of the Annapurna region's wider ecological system and supports birds, plants and other wildlife.

As you climb, vegetation changes according to elevation.

Lower sections are warmer and more humid.

Higher areas support temperate forest and rhododendron.

This provides another visible example of how quickly Himalayan ecosystems change with altitude.
Why this section feels so different
The forest section arrives after the major high-altitude crossing.

The psychological experience changes.

You are descending into warmer conditions.

The trees create shade.

The air contains more moisture.

Birdsong becomes more noticeable.

The trail feels more enclosed than the open upper circuit.

This is one reason the final section can become a highlight in its own right rather than simply a route back to Pokhara.
Discover the Thakali villages and apple orchards
The Kali Gandaki region is closely associated with Thakali culture, and settlements such as Marpha and Tukuche demonstrate the historical relationship between local communities, trade and mountain hospitality.

Marpha is particularly famous for its architecture and apple orchards.

Whitewashed stone buildings line organized pathways.

Traditional settlement design reflects both local construction methods and the need to protect homes from strong valley winds.

Apple cultivation has become an important part of the area's identity.

Visitors may encounter fresh apples, juice, pies, jams and other local products.
The Thakali trading heritage
The Thakali communities developed strong economic relationships with the historic trading routes that passed through the Kali Gandaki.

This history influenced village development.

Trade required organized hospitality.

Caravan routes required food, accommodation and supplies.

Communities positioned along the corridor benefited from facilitating movement.

Modern trekking tourism has changed the scale and character of this economy, but the connection between place and hospitality remains visible.
Marpha as a cultural highlight
Marpha stands out because it combines several aspects of the circuit into one settlement.

It has mountain architecture.

It has agricultural production.

It has a strong food identity.

It has historic trading associations.

And it has a mature teahouse culture.

After the harsh environment around Thorong La and the stark landscapes of the upper Kali Gandaki, Marpha can feel unusually welcoming.

The contrast is part of what makes the stop memorable.
Soak in the hot springs at Tatopani
Tatopani, approximately 1,190 m, is one of the most relaxing highlights of the circuit.

The Nepali name literally means "hot water," and the settlement is associated with natural hot springs where trekkers can rest after the long journey through the high mountains.

The contrast in altitude is substantial.

You may have crossed Thorong La only a few days earlier at 5,416 m.

Now you are back in a warm, green environment where vegetation is dense and temperatures are noticeably higher.
Why Tatopani matters on a long trek
The physical benefits are not the only reason trekkers enjoy Tatopani.

There is a psychological transition taking place.

The highest point of the circuit is behind you.

The most demanding altitude days are over.

The landscape is warmer.

Food choices expand.

Vegetation returns.

You have more opportunities to rest.

The hot springs therefore become a symbolic recovery point.

After days of cold mornings and high-altitude nights, sitting in warm water provides a completely different trekking experience.
Tatopani and the lower Annapurna environment
The area around Tatopani demonstrates just how rapidly the Himalayan climate can change with elevation.

The surrounding landscape is much greener than Muktinath or Jomsom.

Waterfalls and dense vegetation become more common.

Agriculture becomes easier.

The climate begins to resemble the lower foothills rather than the trans-Himalayan plateau.

Tatopani is therefore both a recreational stop and an ecological marker.
Walk the high route through Ghyaru and Ngawal
The high route between Pisang and Manang is one of the major scenic highlights of the Annapurna Circuit.

Instead of remaining on the lower valley trail, trekkers climb toward Ghyaru and Ngawal, gaining elevation to access a broader perspective over the Manang Valley.

The climb is steep.

This is not a route chosen because it is easier.

It is chosen because the additional effort provides a different view of the Annapurna landscape.
Ghyaru
Ghyaru occupies a high position above the valley.

The settlement provides extensive views toward the surrounding mountains.

The higher perspective changes the relationship between the trail and the valley floor.

Instead of seeing the mountains only through a narrow corridor, you can observe the entire landscape unfolding below.

Traditional stone houses and agricultural terraces also reveal how local communities adapted to the high environment.
Ngawal
Ngawal continues the ridge route before the descent toward Manang.

The village has traditional architecture and Buddhist cultural features.

The settlement feels more exposed than the lower valley because of its elevation and surrounding terrain.

For many trekkers, the sequence of Upper Pisang, Ghyaru and Ngawal becomes one of the most photographically rewarding sections of the circuit.

The route also reinforces an important principle of the Annapurna Circuit:
- some of its best views require additional climbing.
Visit the ancient village of Kagbeni
Kagbeni is one of the most distinctive villages on the Annapurna Circuit.

Located near the junction leading toward Upper Mustang, the village has narrow lanes, traditional stone architecture, Buddhist structures and a strong sense of historical continuity.

The settlement's compact form reflects its position in a dry mountain environment and its historical function along the Kali Gandaki corridor.
Kagbeni as a gateway
Kagbeni is often described as a gateway because the route beyond the village leads toward Upper Mustang.

This makes it geographically and culturally significant.

The standard Annapurna Circuit can pass through Kagbeni as part of the normal route.

Travel into Upper Mustang is separately regulated and requires the relevant permit arrangements.

This boundary gives Kagbeni a distinct atmosphere.

You can sense that the landscape is opening toward another region with its own geography and cultural identity.
The old village
The old section of Kagbeni rewards walking rather than simply passing through by vehicle.

Narrow lanes create a maze-like settlement pattern.

Traditional buildings stand close together.

Religious structures and prayer features appear throughout the village.

The surrounding cliffs and barren hills reinforce the sense of being far removed from the green lower valleys.

Kagbeni is therefore one of the strongest examples of how the Annapurna Circuit changes culturally as well as physically.
Experience the diversity of Annapurna's ecosystems
The circuit's ecological diversity is one of its defining strengths.

You begin in a relatively warm environment.

Rice terraces and dense vegetation characterize lower sections.

As elevation increases, the trail enters forest.

Pine, oak, rhododendron and other mountain vegetation become more prominent.

Above Manang, the climate becomes significantly drier.

The landscape changes to sparse alpine vegetation.

Beyond Thorong La, the rain-shadow environment around Muktinath and Mustang becomes even more obvious.

Then, during the descent through the Kali Gandaki, warmer conditions gradually return.

Tatopani becomes lush.

The climb toward Ghorepani brings dense forest.

The route therefore passes through a chain of ecological zones rather than one continuous mountain environment.
Why the rain shadow matters
The Annapurna massif creates major differences in rainfall between its southern and northern sides.

Moisture-laden air from the south rises against the mountains, producing substantial precipitation.

Areas north of the high range receive much less rainfall because they lie partly in the rain shadow.

This helps explain why Manang and Mustang look so dry despite being surrounded by snow-covered mountains.

It also explains why trekkers can experience a completely different climate within the same day's journey when crossing between valleys.
Encounter the rich cultural diversity of the circuit
The Annapurna Circuit is unusual because cultural diversity appears alongside ecological diversity.

The lower areas introduce communities shaped by middle-hill agriculture.

The Manang region reflects Tibetan Buddhist traditions and historic trans-Himalayan trade.

Muktinath combines Hindu and Buddhist pilgrimage traditions.

Kagbeni and the Kali Gandaki reveal older mountain trade settlements.

Thakali villages demonstrate the continuing relationship between trade, hospitality and tourism.
Gurung cultural landscapes
Lower sections of the broader Annapurna region include Gurung settlements and other hill communities.

Terraced agriculture is one of the most visible characteristics.

Traditional houses and village layouts reflect adaptation to the middle-hill environment.

The lower route provides the cultural opening to the much more Tibetan-influenced areas farther north.
Manangi cultural heritage
The Manang region has a distinct cultural identity shaped by geography, Buddhism and trade.

The settlement patterns differ from those of the lower valley.

Stone architecture dominates.

Agriculture is more limited.

Religious features are much more visible.

The history of trade also explains the importance of Manang as a settlement along trans-Himalayan routes.
Thakali heritage
The Thakali communities of the Kali Gandaki are closely associated with hospitality and commerce.

Marpha is the best-known example, but the wider valley contains multiple villages that preserve the architectural and cultural character of the region.

Food is an especially visible expression of this heritage.

Local ingredients and regional dishes reflect the environment and historical trade relationships.

In the morning, you are standing at 5,416 m in a cold alpine environment.

Later, you arrive at a sacred pilgrimage site surrounded by a much drier mountain landscape.

The geographic and cultural contrast occurs within a single day.
The desert and the forest
The movement from Upper Mustang-side terrain toward Tatopani and then Ghorepani is equally dramatic.

The dry landscapes around Kagbeni and Jomsom gradually become greener.

Then the trail reaches warm Tatopani.

After that, the route climbs once again through forest.

The high mountains and the villages
The circuit also balances dramatic mountain scenery with human landscapes.

You are rarely separated from local settlement life for very long.

You pass agricultural areas.

You sleep in teahouses.

You encounter monasteries and religious structures.

You walk through trading villages.

You eat regional food.

You cross roads and bridges used by local residents.

You see how communities have adapted to difficult mountain geography.

This makes the route more than an expedition through scenery.

It becomes a journey through inhabited Himalayan landscapes.
The highlights change with the season
The Annapurna Circuit does not look identical throughout the year.

In spring, the forest sections can become especially colorful as rhododendrons bloom.

In autumn, clearer mountain air often creates excellent visibility, although the trail can be busy.

Winter brings colder conditions and greater snow considerations around Thorong La.

The monsoon influences the wetter southern slopes much more strongly than the rain-shadow side around Manang and Mustang.

This means that the same highlight can feel completely different depending on when you travel.

Annapurna Circuit Trek itinerary: 14 days
The Annapurna Circuit Trek itinerary is designed around a gradual progression from the lower Himalayan foothills to the high-altitude terrain surrounding Manang and Thorong La, followed by a long descent through Muktinath, Mustang and the Kali Gandaki Valley before reaching Tatopani, Ghorepani, Poon Hill and Pokhara.

A well-planned Annapurna Circuit itinerary is not simply a list of villages connected by walking distances. The sequence of sleeping elevations, acclimatization, transportation, daily walking hours, weather exposure and the timing of the Thorong La crossing all influence how the journey feels and how safely it can be completed.

Modern road access has also changed the structure of the circuit. The traditional route began walking much lower in the Marsyangdi Valley, while many current itineraries use a jeep to reach Chame or another higher settlement and reserve the main trekking days for the most scenic and high-altitude sections. The exact starting point therefore depends on the duration of the package and how much of the historic lower trail you want to experience on foot.

This itinerary follows a 14-day Annapurna Circuit Trek structure that combines Kathmandu, the road approach to Chame, the high route through Pisang and Manang, a dedicated acclimatization day, the Thorong La crossing, Muktinath, Jomsom, Tatopani, Ghorepani, Poon Hill, Pokhara and the return to Kathmandu.

The daily schedule is approximate. Walking times can change because of road conditions, weather, trail conditions, individual pace, altitude response and accommodation availability. A mountain itinerary should always retain enough flexibility to make sensible changes when actual conditions differ from the planned schedule.

The full 14-day itinerary below is the base program. Twelve-, sixteen- and longer-day outlines later in this guide are separate alternatives. The base return on Day 13 does not provide a full spare day in Kathmandu before Day 14 departure; add contingency time when booking flights.

The Annapurna Circuit begins long before you take your first trekking step.

Your international arrival in Kathmandu gives you time to recover from travel, organize documents, check trekking equipment and complete the briefing before heading toward the Annapurna region.

A responsible itinerary uses this period to remove avoidable problems before you reach the mountains.

Passport and permit documentation should be checked.

Trekking equipment should be inspected.

Cold-weather clothing should be available before leaving Kathmandu.

Your luggage should be organized so that essential items remain accessible in the daypack.

Route and altitude progression
The Annapurna Circuit Trek route and altitude progression are what give this Himalayan journey its distinctive character. The trail does not simply climb from a low valley to a high point and then return. It moves through the Marsyangdi Valley, rises gradually into the high-altitude landscape of Manang, crosses Thorong La at 5,416 m (17,769 ft), descends into the trans-Himalayan landscape around Muktinath and Mustang, follows the Kali Gandaki Valley and then climbs again through forests toward Ghorepani and Poon Hill.

Nepal Tourism Board describes the classic Annapurna Circuit as a counterclockwise route beginning at Besi Sahar, continuing through the Marsyangdi Valley and Manang, reaching its high point at Thorong La at 5,416 m, and descending through Muktinath toward the Kali Gandaki and the southern Annapurna region.

The route has changed since the circuit became one of Nepal's major trekking destinations. Roads now reach many communities that were historically connected only by foot trails, so modern itineraries often combine walking with jeep transportation. The traditional geographical sequence, however, remains recognizable: Marsyangdi Valley, Manang, Thorong La, Muktinath, Kali Gandaki, Tatopani, Ghorepani and the Poon Hill region.

Altitude is the most important factor to understand when studying the route.

A trekker who looks only at distance may underestimate the difficulty of the upper circuit. The difference between walking 10 kilometers at 2,500 m and walking the same distance around 4,000 m can be substantial because the body is working with less available oxygen. Nepal Tourism Board's mountain-safety guidance recommends that sleeping altitude above 2,500 m should generally increase by no more than 300 to 500 m per day, with a rest day after approximately 1,000 m of altitude gain.

The Annapurna Circuit itinerary therefore works best when the route is understood as an altitude journey, not only a geographical journey.

Besisahar → Chame → Pisang → Manang → Yak Kharka → Thorong Phedi / High Camp → Thorong La → Muktinath → Kagbeni → Jomsom → Marpha → Tukuche → Ghasa → Tatopani → Ghorepani → Poon Hill → Nayapul → Pokhara

Modern road access means that not every trekker walks every one of these sections.

Some trekkers begin at Besisahar and walk deeper into the Marsyangdi Valley.

Others take a jeep to Chame and begin the main trek there.

Some continue by vehicle on the Kali Gandaki side.

Others walk farther through Marpha, Tukuche, Ghasa and Tatopani to preserve more of the classic trail experience.

Nepal Tourism Board continues to describe Besisahar as the traditional starting point and identifies the route's high point as Thorong La at 5,416 m. It also notes that the region now has extensive road access and transport connections, including access through Muktinath and the Kali Gandaki.
The overall altitude pattern of the Annapurna Circuit
The Annapurna Circuit does not have one continuous upward elevation profile.

It has four broad altitude phases.

The first is the lower valley ascent, where trekkers move from the middle hills toward Chame and Pisang.

The second is the high Manang ascent, where sleeping elevations rise into the 3,000 to 4,000 m range.

The third is the Thorong La crossing, where the route reaches its highest point at 5,416 m before descending sharply to Muktinath.

The fourth is the southern return, where the route descends through the Kali Gandaki and Tatopani before climbing again to Ghorepani and Poon Hill.

This is why the Annapurna Circuit is more complicated than a simple altitude chart.

The highest point occurs relatively late.

The route then loses a large amount of elevation.

The trek rises again near Ghorepani.

The final high point at Poon Hill is much lower than Thorong La, but it still involves another climb after the pass crossing.

Thorong La is the highest point of the Annapurna Circuit at 5,416 m. Nepal Tourism Board identifies the pass as the high point of the circuit.

The elevation gain from Thorong Phedi to the summit is substantial.

From approximately 4,450 m to 5,416 m, the route gains nearly 1,000 m in a single day.

At lower elevations, a 1,000-meter climb might be an ambitious but manageable mountain day.

Above 4,000 m, the experience changes.

The air contains less oxygen.

The body's ability to sustain exertion is reduced.

The same physical effort requires more time.

A slow pace is not a sign that something has gone wrong.

It is an expected response to the environment.

The shorter Yak Kharka and Thorong Phedi stages help preserve energy for the crossing.

You cross the pass during the day.

After reaching the highest point, you begin descending.

This is critical because prolonged exposure to extreme altitude is more demanding than a brief summit crossing followed by descent.

The route descends toward Muktinath.

That rapid descent changes the physiological environment immediately.

From the perspective of the altitude profile, Muktinath performs an important role.

Nepal Tourism Board identifies the Kali Gandaki gorge between these major mountain massifs as one of the defining geographical features of the Annapurna region.

The route's altitude continues to decrease.

This makes the southern half of the circuit increasingly different from the upper Manang environment.
Tukuche to Ghasa
As the route continues toward Ghasa, the valley becomes greener.

You are moving into a lower and wetter environment.

The dry highland characteristics gradually disappear.

This is the beginning of another major ecological transition.

The route no longer resembles the landscape around Thorong Phedi.

Trees become increasingly common.

The valley narrows.

Waterfalls and streams become more visible.

The air is warmer.

This is one of the reasons the Annapurna Circuit is so distinctive.

Within a matter of days, the same trekker can experience high-altitude desert-like conditions and dense mountain forest.
Ghasa to Tatopani
The altitude continues dropping toward Tatopani at approximately 1,190 m.

This is one of the largest cumulative altitude drops of the entire circuit.

Compared with Thorong La, Tatopani is more than 4,200 m lower.

That difference is enormous.

The climate changes accordingly.

Vegetation becomes much denser.

Agricultural activity increases.

Temperatures become considerably warmer.

Tatopani is also famous for its natural hot springs, making it one of the most popular recovery points on the extended circuit.

From an altitude perspective, the body is now operating in a completely different environment.

After days above 3,000 and 4,000 m, the lower oxygen stress associated with altitude is dramatically reduced.
Tatopani to Ghorepani: the second major ascent
The altitude profile does not simply continue downward to Pokhara.

This is one of the defining characteristics of the extended Annapurna Circuit.

From Tatopani, the route climbs again toward Ghorepani at approximately 2,860 m.

That means a gain of more than 1,600 m from Tatopani to Ghorepani.

The climb is substantial.

But it is not comparable to the climb toward Thorong La because the starting and finishing elevations are much lower.

The air is denser.

The environment is forested.

The route passes through villages and agricultural terrain.

Rhododendron forest becomes a dominant feature.

The physical challenge is mainly the sustained ascent rather than extreme altitude.
Why the Tatopani-Ghorepani climb is important
This second ascent gives the circuit an unusual altitude profile.

The trek does not have a single summit followed by a permanent descent.

Instead, it crosses a very high mountain pass and later climbs again into another mountain ridge system.

The second climb is lower than Thorong La but provides an entirely different ecological experience.

There is forest.

There are larger trees.

The trail is shaded.

The landscape is greener.

The temperature is warmer.

This makes the Tatopani-Ghorepani section feel like a separate chapter of the same trek.
Ghorepani and the final rise to Poon Hill
Ghorepani stands at approximately 2,860 m.

From there, the route climbs to Poon Hill at approximately 3,210 m.

The elevation gain is modest compared with the earlier stages.

But the climb happens before sunrise.

That changes the experience.

Trekkers leave early while temperatures are cold and the trail is dark.

The objective is to reach the viewpoint before the sun appears over the surrounding Himalayan peaks.

The additional climb is therefore short but strategically timed.

Poon Hill becomes the final major elevation point before the trek returns toward the lower valleys.
Poon Hill is much lower than Thorong La
It is important not to confuse the two high points.

Thorong La reaches 5,416 m.

Poon Hill is approximately 3,210 m.

The difference is more than 2,200 m.

The physical experience is correspondingly different.

At Poon Hill, the main objective is the mountain panorama rather than altitude crossing.

The air is significantly denser.

The route is forested.

The climb is much shorter.

This is why Poon Hill works so well as the final viewpoint of the extended circuit.
Poon Hill to Nayapul
After sunrise, trekkers return toward Ghorepani and continue descending.

The altitude drops through the forest toward Nayapul.

The trail passes through villages and increasingly warm environments.

By Nayapul, the journey has returned close to the lower elevations that characterize the southern gateway of the Annapurna region.

The dramatic altitude cycle is now complete.

You began around the middle hills.

You climbed above 5,000 m.

You descended into Mustang.

You dropped to approximately 1,190 m at Tatopani.

You climbed again to 3,210 m at Poon Hill.

You then descended once more toward the lower valleys.

Sleeping altitude matters more than maximum daytime altitude
When discussing acclimatization, the elevation at which you sleep is particularly important.

For example, a trekker may hike to 4,600 m during the Manang acclimatization day but return to sleep around 3,500 m.

That is very different from immediately sleeping at 4,600 m.

Similarly, crossing Thorong La at 5,416 m does not mean you spend the night at 5,416 m.

You descend to Muktinath.

This distinction matters because the human body responds differently to temporary daytime exposure and sustained overnight exposure.

A sensible Annapurna Circuit itinerary therefore manages sleeping elevations carefully.
How the altitude gains are structured
From Chame onward, the approximate sleeping-altitude gains are:
- Chame to Lower Pisang: approximately +475 m
- Lower Pisang to Manang: approximately +315 m
- Manang to Yak Kharka: approximately +518 m
- Yak Kharka to Thorong Phedi: approximately +432 m
These numbers show the general pattern.

The itinerary does not require several consecutive 1,000-meter sleeping-altitude increases.

The major altitude jump occurs during the daytime crossing of Thorong La.

That is precisely why the acclimatization sequence matters.

Nepal Tourism Board advises that sleeping altitude should not increase more than approximately 300 to 500 m per day above 2,500 m and recommends a rest day after every 1,000 m of gain.
Why altitude progression is more important than distance
A common mistake when comparing trekking itineraries is focusing entirely on kilometers.

Two routes can cover the same distance and have completely different difficulty levels.

Imagine walking 12 km on relatively level terrain at 2,000 m.

Now compare that with walking 12 km involving steep climbing at 4,000 m.

The second day is likely to feel much harder.

Altitude changes the physical cost of movement.

This is why the Annapurna Circuit schedule includes shorter days around Yak Kharka and Thorong Phedi.

The objective is not to maximize mileage.

It is to place the body in the right condition before the pass.
Why Manang is more important than the numbers suggest
Looking only at the altitude table, Manang may appear to be just one more overnight.

It is not.

Manang is the point where the trek pauses the sleeping-altitude progression.

It provides time for adaptation.

It also allows guides to monitor trekkers before the final ascent.

The village's infrastructure makes it easier to spend an additional night than in the smaller high-altitude settlements farther north.

Nepal Tourism Board identifies Manang as an HRA aid-post location during the major trekking seasons, reinforcing its practical importance within the high-altitude section.
What happens if the sleeping altitude increases too quickly?
Rapid ascent can increase the risk of altitude illness.

Nepal Tourism Board identifies altitude illness as potentially life-threatening and specifically recommends gradual ascent above 2,500 m.

Symptoms may include headache, nausea, fatigue, dizziness, sleep disturbance and loss of appetite.

The response should not be to simply push harder.

A trekker experiencing significant or worsening symptoms should inform the guide.

The itinerary may need to stop ascending.

Depending on severity, descending may be necessary.

That is another reason the altitude progression must be treated as a structural part of the trek rather than a detail to be ignored.
How weather interacts with the altitude profile
Altitude does not operate independently of weather.

At lower elevations, rain may slow road travel and make trails slippery.

At higher elevations, snow can affect the Thorong La crossing.

Cold and wind can make an already difficult high-altitude day significantly harder.

The route's different climate zones also mean that the same day can begin in one type of weather and end in another environment.

The Annapurna region is unusual because Manang and Mustang lie in a rain-shadow environment. Nepal Tourism Board notes that these areas are significantly drier than the southern slopes of the Annapurna range.

This explains why the upper route can remain comparatively dry even when monsoon conditions are affecting the southern side.
The route through Manang and Mustang
The altitude progression also explains the cultural transition.

Around Manang, the landscape is high, dry and Tibetan-influenced.

The route then crosses Thorong La.

Muktinath and the upper Kali Gandaki continue the trans-Himalayan character.

As elevation drops toward Jomsom and Marpha, settlement becomes more connected to the river valley and historic trade corridor.

By Tatopani, the environment has returned to much warmer conditions.

The route then climbs through forest rather than desert.

Altitude therefore shapes both the scenery and the human landscape.
Road access and the modern route
Road construction means that the route cannot be described accurately without acknowledging transportation.

Besisahar remains the traditional starting point, but vehicles now serve many sections of the route.

Nepal Tourism Board notes motorable road access on the Muktinath side and describes the Annapurna region as well serviced by transport and teahouses.

For route planning, this means the altitude profile is partly separated from the amount of trail walked.

A trekker may drive from Kathmandu to Chame and begin walking at 2,710 m.

Another trekker may begin around Besisahar and spend several days walking through lower elevations.

Both may ultimately cross Thorong La.

The high-altitude sequence therefore matters regardless of how much of the lower road section is walked.
Classic full walking route versus road-supported route
The classic full walking approach preserves more of the original circuit experience.

You spend more time in the lower Marsyangdi Valley.

You experience a slower ecological transition.

You encounter more villages on foot.

The disadvantage is that the itinerary becomes longer.

The road-supported route allows more time in the higher and more distinctive sections.

It is more practical for travelers with limited vacation time.

But it removes portions of the historic walking experience.

Neither approach changes the central altitude sequence once the route reaches Manang.

The key high-altitude progression remains:

Manang → Yak Kharka → Thorong Phedi / High Camp → Thorong La → Muktinath.
Route variations around Pisang
The route between Pisang and Manang can be adapted according to itinerary.

The lower route has less elevation gain.

The high route through Ghyaru and Ngawal is more demanding but offers broader views.

From the perspective of altitude, the high route adds daytime exposure.

That can be beneficial as part of acclimatization when managed appropriately, but the trekker still needs to return to the planned sleeping altitude.

The decision should be based on route condition and physical condition rather than simply choosing the highest possible trail.
Route variations around Thorong Phedi
The main decision before the pass is whether to sleep at Thorong Phedi or High Camp.

Thorong Phedi:
- Lower sleeping altitude.
- Longer climb the next morning.
- More conservative overnight elevation.
- High Camp:
- Higher sleeping altitude.
- Shorter climb to the pass.
- Potentially more difficult sleep because of elevation.
The correct choice depends on the individual and the circumstances.

A trekking operator should not use High Camp automatically simply to make the itinerary appear shorter.
Route variations after Jomsom
The route can also change after Jomsom.

One option is to continue south by road.

Another is to walk through Marpha, Tukuche, Ghasa and Tatopani.

Another possibility is a flight from Jomsom to Pokhara, subject to operational and weather conditions.

The altitude profile changes dramatically depending on which option is selected.

Walking south preserves the descent through the Kali Gandaki and the Tatopani-Ghorepani section.

Flying removes that entire southern walking sequence.

For travelers wanting the fullest circuit experience, preserving the lower Kali Gandaki and Ghorepani section adds significant geographical diversity.
Route extensions and altitude
The circuit can also be extended with side trips that change the altitude profile.

Tilicho Lake, at approximately 4,919 m, is one of the most prominent additions.

Nepal Tourism Board identifies Lake Tilicho as approximately 4,919 m within the Manang region.

Adding Tilicho means reaching another high elevation before the Thorong La crossing.

The additional altitude exposure and extra walking days need to be incorporated into the itinerary.

The circuit can also be combined with routes toward Annapurna Base Camp or other trekking areas, but these combinations create entirely different durations and altitude profiles.

For a standard Annapurna Circuit package, the core sequence should remain clear.
Altitude and acclimatization are linked to daily pacing
A route can have a theoretically correct altitude profile and still be poorly designed if the daily walking pace is too aggressive.

The goal is not simply to sleep at the correct elevation.

It is to reach each overnight destination without excessive exhaustion.

This is why the upper circuit contains shorter walking days.

A trekker who arrives at Yak Kharka completely exhausted may be less prepared for the next day's climb.

A trekker who arrives at Thorong Phedi late in the afternoon after unnecessary exertion may have less opportunity to rest.

A good route plan therefore coordinates:
- Sleeping altitude.
- Walking duration.
- Elevation gain.
- Rest periods.
- Meal timing.
- Weather conditions.
- Pass-crossing timing.
These elements must work together.

Difficulty, fitness and physical preparation
The Annapurna Circuit Trek difficulty comes from a combination of altitude, sustained walking, steep climbs and descents, changing weather, uneven mountain terrain and the physical and mental demands of crossing Thorong La Pass at 5,416 m (17,769 ft).

The Annapurna Circuit is not a technical mountaineering expedition. Under normal seasonal conditions, trekkers do not need advanced climbing skills, ropes or technical mountaineering equipment for the standard route. The difficulty comes from spending many consecutive days walking through a high-altitude mountain environment and eventually crossing one of the highest established trekking passes in the Himalaya.

Nepal Tourism Board advises trekkers heading into mountain areas above 2,500 m to understand Acute Mountain Sickness, ascend gradually and follow appropriate safety procedures. Its current mountain-safety guidance recommends limiting sleeping-altitude gains to approximately 300 to 500 m per day above 2,500 m and taking a rest day after approximately 1,000 m of cumulative sleeping-altitude gain.

The CDC Yellow Book 2026 similarly emphasizes gradual acclimatization and notes that physical fitness does not protect travelers from altitude illness. Once above 3,000 m, the CDC recommends increasing sleeping altitude by no more than approximately 500 m per day and adding an extra acclimatization night for every additional 1,000 m of sleeping-altitude gain.

This distinction is important when considering the Annapurna Circuit.

You can be physically fit and still experience altitude symptoms.

You can be an experienced hiker and still need to slow down at 4,000 m.

You can walk long distances comfortably at home and discover that the same effort feels substantially harder at high altitude.

At the same time, proper physical preparation can make the trekking days considerably more manageable. Strong legs, good cardiovascular endurance, muscular endurance, balance and familiarity with long uphill and downhill walking all reduce the amount of avoidable physical stress during the journey.

The goal of preparation is not to make the Annapurna Circuit easy.

The goal is to make your body sufficiently prepared that altitude and terrain, rather than basic lack of fitness, are the main variables you have to manage.
How difficult is the Annapurna Circuit Trek?
The Annapurna Circuit is best described as a challenging high-altitude trek.

The standard route involves repeated uphill and downhill walking over many consecutive days, with the highest point at Thorong La reaching 5,416 m.

Most trekking days can involve approximately 5 to 7 hours of walking, although some are shorter and the Thorong La crossing can become substantially longer depending on the starting point, weather, pace and trail conditions.

The difficulty comes from several factors working together.

The first is altitude.

The second is duration.

The third is elevation gain and loss.

The fourth is terrain.

The fifth is weather.

The sixth is the psychological challenge of completing the Thorong La crossing after several days of accumulated fatigue.

None of these factors should be considered in isolation.

A steep climb at 2,000 m is challenging.

A similar climb at 4,000 m can feel considerably harder because less oxygen is available.

A long descent may not be difficult from a cardiovascular perspective, but it can place considerable stress on the quadriceps, knees and calves.

A cold, windy morning at high altitude can increase the difficulty even when the actual distance is moderate.

The Annapurna Circuit therefore rewards endurance more than speed.
The difficulty changes throughout the route
The lower section of the circuit is generally easier from an altitude perspective.

Around Chame and Pisang, trekkers are already climbing, but the oxygen limitation is much less severe than above Manang.

The major transition occurs around Manang.

Once the route moves toward Yak Kharka, the elevation becomes one of the dominant factors affecting walking pace.

Around 4,000 m, even short uphill sections can require frequent pauses.

By Thorong Phedi and High Camp, the combination of altitude, cold and terrain becomes more demanding.

The pass itself is the culmination.

Then the difficulty changes again.

After crossing Thorong La, the route descends toward Muktinath.

This reduces altitude but creates a long downhill walking challenge.

Later, the extended itinerary climbs again from Tatopani toward Ghorepani.

That climb occurs at much lower elevation than Thorong La, but it can still be tiring because it follows a long trek.

The physical challenge therefore changes rather than disappearing.
The three main physical challenges of the Annapurna Circuit
The difficulty of the Annapurna Circuit can be reduced to three primary physical demands: endurance, elevation and recovery.

Endurance allows you to walk for several hours.

Elevation makes that walking more demanding.

Recovery determines whether you can repeat the effort the following day.

These three factors interact throughout the trek.

A trekker with strong cardiovascular fitness but poor leg strength may struggle on long descents.

A trekker with strong legs but insufficient endurance may struggle on long trekking days.

A trekker who is physically fit in both areas may still experience altitude illness if the ascent is too rapid for their individual physiology.

That is why fitness and acclimatization must be treated as separate issues.
Endurance
The Annapurna Circuit requires the ability to remain active for several consecutive hours.

This is different from running a short race or completing a single hard gym workout.

You need to walk uphill.

Walk on flatter ground.

Descend.

Stop.

Restart.

Walk again.

Then repeat the same process the next day.

This repetitive muscular and cardiovascular demand is one of the defining characteristics of long-distance trekking.
Elevation
Altitude becomes increasingly significant above approximately 2,500 m.

The CDC notes that any previously unacclimatized traveler sleeping at approximately 2,450 m or higher can be at risk of altitude illness, with risk influenced heavily by elevation of entry and rate of ascent.

The Annapurna Circuit therefore cannot be trained for entirely at sea level or low elevation.

You can prepare your cardiovascular and muscular systems through ordinary training.

You cannot reproduce the physiological effects of 5,000 m simply by exercising harder at low altitude.
- Recovery
The circuit is a multi-day effort.

Your legs need to recover every night.

Your body needs enough food and fluid.

Sleep matters.

Pacing matters.

A person who spends the first few days walking too aggressively may enter the Manang-to-Thorong La section already fatigued.

This is why the itinerary should not be treated as a competition.

The goal is to arrive at Thorong Phedi with enough physical reserve for the pass day.
The Thorong La crossing deserves special preparation
The Thorong La crossing is the most demanding physical section of the Annapurna Circuit.

The pass reaches 5,416 m.

The route from Thorong Phedi involves a major climb.

The crossing begins early.

Cold and wind may affect conditions.

The trail can be snowy or icy depending on the season.

The route then descends substantially toward Muktinath.

The difficulty therefore comes from the complete day rather than from the summit elevation alone.

From Thorong Phedi, the climb toward the pass can involve approximately 1,000 m of elevation gain.

After reaching the summit, the route loses roughly another 1,500 to 1,600 m toward Muktinath.

This means the legs must manage both sustained uphill climbing and prolonged downhill movement.
Why the Thorong La day feels so long
At high elevation, walking speed naturally decreases.

The body is exposed to reduced oxygen availability.

A pace that feels comfortable at 2,500 m may feel too fast at 5,000 m.

This is normal.

Trying to force your lower-elevation walking speed can create unnecessary fatigue.

The correct strategy is to slow down.

Take regular short breaks.

Drink regularly.

Eat small amounts of food throughout the day.

Keep warm.

Follow the guide's pace.

The goal is consistent movement rather than speed.
The descent is part of the challenge
Trekkers often focus almost entirely on the climb toward Thorong La.

The descent deserves equal preparation.

Going downhill for several hours places repeated eccentric load on the quadriceps.

The knees and ankles also work continuously to control each step.

Uneven terrain increases the demand on balance.

Trekking poles can be particularly useful during this portion.

Your physical training should therefore include downhill walking.

A trekker who only trains on flat roads or machines may be unprepared for the muscular demands of a long mountain descent.
What level of fitness do you need?
You do not need elite athletic fitness to complete the Annapurna Circuit.

You do need a solid level of trekking fitness.

A useful practical benchmark is the ability to complete long day hikes involving several hours of continuous movement, including uphill and downhill terrain, without excessive fatigue.

You should also be able to repeat that effort on consecutive days.

Your training should gradually develop four main capacities:
- Cardiovascular endurance
- Leg strength
- Muscular endurance
- Balance and core stability
These systems work together throughout the trek.
Cardiovascular endurance
Cardiovascular fitness helps you continue walking without becoming excessively breathless from ordinary exertion.

Useful training activities include:
- Brisk walking.
- Hill walking.
- Running.
- Cycling.
- Stair climbing.
- Incline treadmill walking.
- Long hiking sessions.
The most trek-specific option is hiking because it reproduces the movement pattern and terrain variability more closely than many gym activities.
Leg strength
The legs carry the majority of the physical load during the Annapurna Circuit.

The major muscles involved include the quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes and calves.

Strong legs are particularly important during:
- Long climbs.
- Steep descents.
- Uneven terrain.
The Thorong La crossing.

The descent toward Muktinath.

The Tatopani-Ghorepani climb.

Useful exercises include:
- Squats.
- Lunges.
- Step-ups.
- Split squats.
- Calf raises.
- Single-leg exercises.
- Controlled downhill step exercises.
The purpose is not bodybuilding.

The purpose is improving functional strength for repeated mountain movement.
- Muscular Endurance
Strength and endurance are different.

You may be able to perform heavy squats once or twice while still struggling to walk uphill for several hours.

The Annapurna Circuit requires muscles to continue working repeatedly.

That means training should eventually include higher-repetition strength work and longer hiking sessions.

Back-to-back hiking days are particularly useful because they replicate the recovery demands of the actual trek.

For example, one longer hike on Saturday followed by another moderate hike on Sunday trains the body to perform while not fully recovered.
Core stability
Core stability matters when carrying a daypack and moving across irregular ground.

A strong core helps maintain posture and balance.

Useful exercises include:
- Planks.
- Side planks.
- Bird dogs.
- Controlled mountain climbers.
- Single-leg balance work.
The goal is not to create a specialized fitness routine.

The goal is to make uneven trail movement more efficient.
How long should you train before the Annapurna Circuit?
For a person starting from average fitness, 8 to 12 weeks of structured preparation provides a practical training window.

Someone already accustomed to long-distance hiking may need less specific preparation.

Someone who rarely exercises may need more gradual progression.

The most important factor is consistency.

Training should build progressively rather than suddenly increasing workload immediately before departure.
Weeks 1 to 4: build the base
The first phase should establish consistent cardiovascular activity.

Focus on regular walking, moderate hill work, stairs, cycling or running.

Add basic leg-strength exercises.

The goal is to make regular physical activity habitual rather than exhausting yourself.

Descending from Thorong La to Muktinath is long.

What about running?
Running can be useful for cardiovascular fitness.

How to train at elevation
Most trekkers cannot realistically spend weeks training at 5,000 m before traveling to Nepal.

Training at high elevation is therefore not a requirement.

What cannot be replicated through ordinary exercise is acclimatization.

This means a strong athlete still needs a sensible ascent schedule.

That is why the itinerary is structured around gradual ascent and a Manang acclimatization day.
Fitness does not prevent altitude sickness
This is one of the most important principles to understand before the Annapurna Circuit.

Being fit does not guarantee protection against Acute Mountain Sickness.

The CDC states directly that training and physical fitness do not affect altitude-illness risk. Risk depends heavily on acclimatization, altitude and ascent rate, with individual susceptibility also playing a role.

This explains why two trekkers with similar fitness can have very different experiences above 4,000 m.

One may feel comfortable.

The other may develop a headache, nausea, dizziness or unusual fatigue.

Neither result necessarily reflects fitness.

Altitude is an environmental stressor.

It must be managed separately from physical preparation.
Understanding the difference between fatigue and altitude symptoms
Normal trekking fatigue is expected.

Your legs can feel tired after climbing.

Your shoulders may feel sore from a backpack.

Your feet may feel tired after several hours.

Altitude illness presents differently.

Nepal Tourism Board lists early AMS symptoms including headache, loss of appetite, nausea, fatigue on minimal exertion and dizziness.

The symptoms matter particularly when they appear after ascending.

A trekker should not assume every symptom is simply tiredness.

Communication with the guide is essential.
What should you do when you feel unwell at altitude?
Nepal Tourism Board advises trekkers who are unwell above 2,500 m to consider AMS until proven otherwise. Its current guidance recommends not going higher when symptoms are present, resting and monitoring the situation, with descent required if symptoms worsen.

The CDC similarly recommends not ascending to a higher sleeping altitude when symptoms are present and emphasizes descent when symptoms worsen despite remaining at the same elevation.

This is important for physical preparation because the strongest trekker in the group is not automatically the person who should continue.

The appropriate response to altitude symptoms is not more effort.

It is appropriate altitude management.
Hydration and nutrition during the trek
Physical preparation continues after you arrive in Nepal.

Eating enough is important because the circuit involves sustained activity.

The upper sections can reduce appetite, making it easy to under-eat.

Practical trekking foods such as dal bhat, rice, noodles, potatoes, soups and other carbohydrate-rich dishes provide useful energy.

Hydration should also be maintained.

Nepal Tourism Board advises trekkers to drink clean water and avoid alcohol while managing altitude.

The CDC recommends avoiding alcohol during the initial acclimatization period at high altitude because alcohol and other respiratory depressants can interfere with acclimatization and sleep.

Hydration does not prevent altitude illness by itself.

It supports general physical function.

Sleep quality can become worse as altitude increases.

A trekker who sleeps poorly at 4,000 m and then completes a long day may accumulate fatigue quickly.

Use the evening to recover.

Hydrate.

Prepare your equipment.

Keep sleeping clothing dry.

Avoid unnecessary nighttime exertion.

The goal is to conserve physical energy for the following day.
Why pacing matters more than speed
One of the most common physical mistakes on a Himalayan trek is walking too quickly in the lower sections and assuming that the same pace should be maintained at altitude.

It should not.

The guide may deliberately establish a slow walking rhythm.

The goal is controlled movement.
