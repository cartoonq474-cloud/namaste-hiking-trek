Expedition Overview

Rising to 6,476 m (21,247 ft) above sea level, Mera Peak Central stands proudly as the highest officially designated trekking peak in Nepal. Nestled in the dramatic Solukhumbu and Makalu-Barun borderlands, this extraordinary 18-day expedition bridges the gap between classic high-altitude trekking and true Himalayan mountaineering. It offers adventurous trekkers and aspiring alpinists the ultimate opportunity to reach a genuine Himalayan summit without requiring advanced technical climbing experience.

What truly sets the Mera Peak expedition apart is its route. Unlike the heavily traveled highways of the Everest Base Camp corridor, our journey ventures south from Lukla through the secluded, wild trails of the Hinku Valley. Walking through ancient rhododendron and bamboo cloud forests, across rushing torrents, and through traditional Rai and Sherpa hamlets such as Paiya and Pangkongma, you experience an authentic side of Nepal that few trekkers ever witness. As you ascend, the forests give way to alpine pastures, tumbling moraines, and the towering granite spires of Peak 41, Kyashar, and Mera itself.

What is the Mera Peak Climbing Experience Really Like?

Mera Peak is officially graded Alpine PD (Peu Difficile / Slightly Difficult) on the international mountaineering scale. While the non-technical glaciated slopes rarely exceed 30 to 35 degrees, the immense altitude, sub-zero temperatures, and thin air present a formidable physical and mental challenge. At 6,476 m, the barometric pressure delivers less than half the atmospheric oxygen available at sea level.

To ensure safety and success, our 18-day itinerary is meticulously paced. We follow a gentle, circular acclimatization approach via Pangkongma La (3,174 m) and Kothe (3,600 m) before reaching Khare Base Camp (5,045 m). Here, under the dedicated guidance of our UIAGM/NMA-certified Climbing Sherpas, you spend a full training day mastering glacier travel skills: practicing crampon technique on real ice, handling fixed ropes with jumar ascenders, utilizing figure-eight descenders, and synchronizing tied-in rope team movement.

The Unrivaled 360-Degree Summit Panorama

The crowning jewel of Mera Peak is undeniably its summit vantage point. Positioned strategically east of the Khumbu range, the central summit offers what mountaineering historians widely agree is the finest mountain panorama anywhere in the Himalaya. As the morning sun pierces the horizon, five of the earth’s fourteen 8,000-meter giants ignite in golden light:

  • Mount Everest (8,848.86 m): Towering dominantly to the north over the Lhotse wall.
  • Lhotse (8,516 m): Flanking Everest with its sheer south face glowing crimson.
  • Makalu (8,481 m): Rising as an immense four-sided granite pyramid to the immediate northeast.
  • Cho Oyu (8,188 m): Anchoring the northern skyline near the Tibetan border.
  • Kanchenjunga (8,586 m): The world’s third-highest peak shimmering majestically on the far eastern horizon.

In addition to these five titans, you stand surrounded by celebrated peaks including Ama Dablam (6,812 m), Baruntse (7,129 m), Chamlang (7,319 m), and Naulekha. It is a vista that burns permanently into your memory, making every step of the strenuous ascent profoundly rewarding.

Is the Mera Peak Climbing Expedition Right for You?

This expedition is tailor-made for physically fit trekkers seeking their first Himalayan summit, seasoned hillwalkers ready for a greater challenge, and mountaineers looking to build acclimatization and high-altitude endurance before tackling 7,000 m or 8,000 m peaks. Previous technical mountaineering experience is not mandatory, but solid cardiovascular stamina, mental perseverance, and prior trekking experience at altitudes above 4,000 meters are strongly recommended.

The feeling of stepping onto the narrow summit crest alongside your dedicated Climbing Sherpa, roped together as a unified team, is a life-affirming milestone that validates your physical endurance, mental perseverance, and passion for the high mountains.

The World-Renowned 360° Panorama of 5 Eight-Thousanders

Everest: 8,848 m Kanchenjunga: 8,586 m Lhotse: 8,516 m Makalu: 8,481 m Cho Oyu: 8,188 m

Mera Peak Central commands what mountaineering historians widely recognize as the finest panoramic view in the entire Himalaya. Because Mera sits slightly south and east of the main Khumbu ridge, your vantage point offers an unobstructed, stadium-like view encompassing five of the world's six highest peaks simultaneously.

Looking north, Mount Everest (8,848.86 m) towers above the immense Nuptse-Lhotse wall, flanked by Lhotse (8,516 m) and Cho Oyu (8,188 m). To the immediate east, the four-sided granite pyramid of Makalu (8,481 m) looms dramatically, while far on the eastern horizon, the massive massif of Kanchenjunga (8,586 m) glistens above Sikkim. Below you, the sheer rock faces of Chamlang, Baruntse, and Ama Dablam complete an amphitheater unmatched anywhere on Earth.

Trek the Secluded Trails of the Hinku Valley Wilderness

Region: Solukhumbu & Makalu-Barun Environment: Virgin Cloud Forest Crowd Level: Low / Off-the-Beaten-Path

While thousands of trekkers crowd the commercial tea trail toward Everest Base Camp, our Mera Peak route branches south into the remote, peaceful sanctuary of the Hinku Valley. This pristine wilderness preserves the authentic soul of Himalayan trekking as it existed decades ago, characterized by silence, raw granite canyons, and untouched biodiversity.

The trail winds beneath towering hemlocks, moss-draped silver firs, and blooming rhododendron forests before emerging into glacial river valleys bordered by vertical 2,000-meter cliffs. You cross clear torrents over wooden bridges and walk along trails where the only sounds are rushing glacial water, the rustle of wind through high bamboo, and the occasional call of the Himalayan monal pheasant.

Hands-On Alpine Glacier Clinic at Khare (5,045 m)

Location: Khare Alpine Base Day: Dedicated Day 12 Mentors: UIAGM/NMA Climbing Sherpas

No prior mountaineering experience is necessary to join this expedition because Day 12 is completely dedicated to comprehensive, practical alpine skills instruction. Conducted on real moraine ice slopes above Khare Base Camp (5,045 m), our certified climbing Sherpas teach you essential mountaineering techniques with patience and precision.

Climbers practice fitting double mountaineering boots, walking in steel 12-point crampons (both flat-footing French technique and front-pointing), executing self-arrest with an ice axe, operating mechanical jumar ascenders on fixed ropes, and controlled abseiling using figure-8 descenders. By the time you step onto the Mera Glacier, every technical movement is familiar and confident.

Sleep Suspended Above the Clouds at Mera High Camp (5,780 m)

High Camp Alt: 5,780 m / 18,963 ft Shelter: 4-Season Geodesic Tents Night Temp: -15°C to -22°C

Perched dramatically behind a rocky promontory on the Mera Glacier edge, Mera High Camp (5,780 m) ranks among the highest sleeping locations on Earth. Setting camp here shortens summit day significantly, giving our team a decisive tactical advantage compared to operators attempting the summit from Khare.

Sheltered inside rugged Mountain Hardwear and North Face 4-season geodesic dome tents with closed-cell insulating mattresses, you stay warm while our Sherpas serve hot soups and high-carbohydrate meals. As dusk settles, step outside your tent to watch an unforgettable sunset turn Mount Everest, Lhotse, Makalu, and Chamlang into glowing crimson spires above an endless ocean of clouds.

The Thrilling 2:00 AM Headlamp Summit Push Under Starlight

Alpine Start: 02:00 AM Pacing: Roped Guided Team Summit Duration: 9 to 11 Hours

Awakening at 1:30 AM to steaming tea and hot porridge inside your high-camp tent, the anticipation is palpable. After strapping on crampons, securing climbing harnesses, and switching on headlamps, you step into the sub-zero Himalayan night under a breathtaking dome of diamond-like stars.

Roped together in teams of 2 to 3 with certified Climbing Sherpas, the team maintains a steady, rhythmic pressure-breathing pace across the wide central glacier bowl. The crunch of steel crampons on frozen crust and the beams of headlamps carving through the darkness create an indelible memory. As dawn approaches, the eastern sky slowly morphs from midnight indigo to blazing gold, illuminating the immense south face of Kanchenjunga.

The Exhilarating 40-Meter Headwall & Fixed-Rope Ascent

Slope Gradient: 40° to 45° Technical Gear: Jumar Ascender & Safety Sling Safety: Fixed Safety Line

While the majority of Mera Peak is a gentle 25°–30° snow plod, the climb reaches a thrilling climax at the final summit headwall. Rising approximately 40 to 50 meters beneath the apex dome, the slope steepens to an exhilarating 40°–45° angle of hard-packed snow and alpine ice.

Here, our lead Climbing Sherpas fix a robust dynamic safety line anchored with snow stakes and ice screws. Clipping your mechanical ascender (jumar) and safety tether to the line, you front-point up the final ridge with complete peace of mind. Topping out onto the razor crest and stepping onto the central summit dome provides an unforgettable rush of triumph and emotion.

Gradual Southern Acclimatization Loop via Paiya & Pangkongma

Route Strategy: Southern Valley Approach Acclimatization: 5 Days Below 3,600 m Summit Success: >92%

Many low-cost operators rush clients straight over the 4,610m Zatrwa La Pass on Day 2 directly from Lukla, triggering severe Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) and forcing premature evacuations. Our 18-day itinerary adopts a physiologically proven circular route that heads south from Lukla through Paiya (2,730 m) and Pangkongma (2,846 m).

By spending five full days gaining elevation gradually below 3,600 meters, your body synthesizes essential red blood cells and adjusts smoothly to high altitude. When you finally reach Kothe and Khare, you arrive energetic, well-fed, and properly acclimatized, dramatically boosting your personal probability of summit success.

Traverse the High Rocky Saddle of Zatrwa La Pass (4,610 m)

Pass Altitude: 4,610 m / 15,124 ft Stage: Day 17 Return Traverse Terrain: Alpine Moraine & Boulder Saddle

Rather than retracing the long southern loop back through Pangkongma, our expedition delivers a spectacular final alpine challenge by crossing the dramatic Zatrwa La Pass (4,610 m) on Day 17. Because your body is now thoroughly acclimatized from climbing above 6,400 meters, crossing this rugged pass feels empowering and exhilarating.

The rocky traverse out of Thuli Kharka rewards you with dual valley panoramas looking across the Hinku river gorge on one side and the deep Dudh Koshi basin on the other. Adorned with fluttering Buddhist prayer flags, the pass marks your triumphant return crossing toward Lukla for an evening of hot showers and celebratory banquets.

Hike to Sabai Tsho Glacial Lake & Charpate Ridge (5,000 m)

Acclimatization Alt: 5,000 m Feature: Turquoise Glacial Lake Mountain View: Kyashar North Face

During our active acclimatization day in Thangnak (Day 10), we embark on a spectacular side excursion following the lateral moraine of the Dig Glacier up toward the turquoise waters of Sabai Tsho lake and the high vantage points of Charpate Ridge (5,000 m).

This hike exemplifies the golden mountaineering principle: climb high, sleep low. Spending hours adjusting at 5,000 meters before returning to sleep at Thangnak (4,356 m) primes your vascular system. Furthermore, the vantage point places you directly opposite the sheer 2,000-meter north face of Kyashar (Peak 43) and the tumbling icefalls of Mera Glacier.

Visit the Ancient 200-Year-Old Lungsumgba Rock Gompa

Heritage: 200-Year-Old Tibetan Shrine Tradition: Butter Lamp Puja Culture: Rai & Sherpa Mountain Lore

Between Kothe and Thangnak, nestled beneath an immense granite boulder overhang, lies the sacred 200-year-old Buddhist sanctuary of Lungsumgba Gompa. Carved directly into the rock face and painted with traditional Tibetan Buddhist iconography, this ancient hermitage has blessed generations of Himalayan mountaineers.

Climbing Sherpas and trekkers pause here to light brass butter lamps, hang prayer flags, and pray for safe mountain passage and favorable weather. Meeting local lamas and experiencing this deeply spiritual mountain ritual adds profound cultural depth to your physical journey through the Hinku Valley.

Activity: High alpine ridge walking above tree line
Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner
Overnight: Chunbu Kharka (4,200 m)

Today we break through the tree line into the true high-alpine zone. The trail winds along rocky ridgelines with expansive vistas stretching east toward the Kangchenjunga range and north toward the glistening summits of the Barun and Hinku mountains.

We pass sacred alpine tarns and the rugged trails leading toward the sacred pilgrimage lakes of Panch Pokhari. The terrain becomes rockier and more exposed, with icy streams carving through glacial boulders.

We reach Chunbu Kharka at 4,200 m. Sleeping above 4,000 meters for the first time on the expedition is a vital physiological milestone. Our guides conduct evening pulse oximetry readings, ensure warm herbal tea intake, and instruct everyone on breathing and thermal management.

Day 8: Trek from Chunbu Kharka Down to Kothe into the Main Hinku Valley

Chunbu Kharka to Kothe – approximately 3,600 m / 11,811 ft

Walking time: 6 to 7 hours
Distance: ~10 km | Elevation change: +200 m / -800 m
Accommodation: Mountain Teahouse Lodge
Activity: Descent into the main Hinku River canyon
Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner
Overnight: Kothe (3,600 m)

We depart Chunbu Kharka and navigate rocky moraine trails before undertaking a long descent through stunted juniper scrub and moss-carpeted birch woodlands into the main glacial valley of the Hinku River.

As we approach the valley floor, the roaring Hinku Khola emerges, tumbling over gigantic granite boulders. The canyon walls soar thousands of meters overhead, topped by shimmering hanging glaciers and granite pillars.

We arrive in Kothe (3,600 m), the primary staging village of the Hinku Valley. Dropping back down to 3,600 meters provides welcome physiological relief, allowing your blood cells to replenish oxygen reserves efficiently. Settle into a comfortable riverside teahouse for hearty dal bhat and a restful sleep.

Day 9: Trek along the Hinku River from Kothe to Thangnak

Kothe to Thangnak – approximately 4,356 m / 14,291 ft

Walking time: 4 to 5 hours

Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner
Overnight: Thangnak (4,356 m)

Following the west bank of the Hinku Khola, the trail heads north deeper into the high mountains. The scenery shifts dramatically as forested slopes recede, giving way to monumental U-shaped glacial valleys bordered by jagged peaks like Kusum Kanguru (6,367 m) and Kyashar (6,770 m).

Along the trail, we stop at the 200-year-old Lungsumgba Gompa, a sacred Buddhist shrine carved beneath an enormous rock overhang. Climbers and Sherpas traditionally light butter lamps and pray here for a safe and auspicious mountain ascent.

Continuing past wide lateral moraines, we arrive at Thangnak (4,356 m), a seasonal settlement nestled at the foot of Charpate Himal. Thangnak serves as our primary acclimatization base before pushing into the extreme alpine zone.

Day 10: Acclimatization & Exploration Day in Thangnak (Hike to Charpate Lake 5,000m)

Thangnak – 4,356 m / 14,291 ft (Hike to 5,000 m)

Hiking time: 4 to 5 hours
Elevation gain: +644 m / -644 m
Accommodation: Mountain Teahouse Lodge
Activity: Active acclimatization hike to Sabai Tsho glacial lake
Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner
Overnight: Thangnak (4,356 m)

To ensure your body synthesizes essential red blood cells and adapts smoothly to the thinning atmosphere, today is an active acclimatization day following the golden mountaineering rule: 'Climb high, sleep low.'

After a relaxed breakfast, we take an acclimatization hike up the lateral moraine of the Dig Glacier toward the turquoise waters of Sabai Tsho glacial lake and the viewpoints around Charpate Himal (5,000 m). The vista of Mera's glaciers and the towering north face of Kyashar is awe-inspiring.

We spend an hour at the high viewpoint soaking in the panoramic grandeur and allowing our bodies to adjust, before leisurely descending back to Thangnak for a hot lunch, hydration, and an afternoon of resting and gear organization.

Day 11: Trek from Thangnak to Khare / Mera Base Camp (5,045m)

Thangnak to Khare – approximately 5,045 m / 16,551 ft

Walking time: 4 to 5 hours
Distance: ~6 km | Elevation change: +689 m
Accommodation: High-Altitude Teahouse Lodge
Activity: Moraine traverse into Mera Alpine Base Camp
Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner
Overnight: Khare (5,045 m)

From Thangnak, we follow the lateral moraine of the Dig Glacier, climbing steadily toward the snout of the Hinku Nup glacier. The trail crosses boulder-strewn moraine fields with views of hanging glaciers cascading off the Mera wall.

As we climb higher, the colossal bulk of Mera Peak Central (6,476 m) and Mera North dominates the northern sky. The air feels noticeably thinner, demanding a deliberate, rhythmic walking pace and controlled breathing.

We arrive in Khare (5,045 m), the bustling high-altitude staging camp for all Mera Peak climbs. Khare features several well-equipped stone lodges, bakeries, and gear rental shops. You will check into your lodge, enjoy warm garlic soup (renowned for altitude acclimatization), and take in the dramatic icefall looming directly above the settlement.

Day 12: Pre-Climbing Glacier Training & Rest Day in Khare (5,045m)

Khare – 5,045 m / 16,551 ft

Training duration: 3 to 4 hours
Accommodation: High-Altitude Teahouse Lodge
Activity: Glacier ropes, crampons & jumar ascending clinic
Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner
Overnight: Khare (5,045 m)

Today is a pivotal day dedicated to your summit readiness. After breakfast, our certified Climbing Sherpas lead you to a nearby glacier slope or steep moraine ice pitch for comprehensive hands-on alpine skills training.

You will practice: (1) Securing double mountaineering boots and strapping crampons securely, (2) French and front-pointing crampon walking techniques, (3) Self-arrest techniques using your ice axe, (4) Ascending steep fixed lines using a mechanical ascender (jumar) and safety sling, and (5) Controlled abseiling (rappelling) with a figure-eight descender.

Our guides also simulate rope-team glacier travel, emphasizing communication, proper rope tension, and crevasse awareness. The afternoon is spent resting, drinking abundant fluids, and packing your summit duffel and summit daypack with essentials (thermal layers, headlamp, extra lithium batteries, insulated thermos flask).

Day 13: Trek from Khare to Mera Peak High Camp (5,780m) via Mera La (5,415m)

Khare to High Camp – approximately 5,780 m / 18,963 ft

We depart Khare climbing up boulder fields and steep scree toward the Mera Glacier snout. Here, we strap on our crampons, rope up in guided teams, and step onto the vast, gleaming ice of the Mera Glacier.

The ascent up to Mera La Pass (5,415 m) is a steady, gentle glacier plod over firm snowfields. Reaching the pass opens up breathtaking views of the Hongu Valley and the formidable south face of Mount Chamlang (7,319 m).

From Mera La, we continue ascending along the glaciated slope to Mera High Camp (5,780 m), nestled behind a rocky promontory that provides shelter from prevailing winds. Our Sherpa team has already erected sturdy 4-season geodesic expedition tents. As evening arrives, witness one of the planet's most spectacular sunsets, with Everest, Lhotse, Makalu, and Baruntse glowing in deep crimson light.

Enjoy a hot, calorie-dense dinner, fill thermoses with boiling water, and retire early into your sub-zero sleeping bag for a few hours of rest before the midnight wake-up call.

Day 14: Summit Push: Mera Peak Central (6,476m) & Descent to Khare (5,045m)

High Camp to Summit (6,476 m) & Return to Khare (5,045 m)

Push time: 9 to 11 hours round trip
Distance: ~10 km | Elevation change: +696 m / -1,431 m
Accommodation: High-Altitude Teahouse Lodge
Activity: 2:00 AM alpine summit push & 5x 8,000m panorama
Meals: Light Breakfast, Summit Snacks, Hot Lunch & Dinner
Overnight: Khare (5,045 m)

Summit Day! The wake-up call comes at 1:30 AM. After hot porridge and steaming tea, we gear up: double boots, crampons, harness, windproof down layers, and headlamps. Roped together with our Climbing Sherpas (1:2 ratio), we step into the sub-zero darkness under an infinite sea of stars around 2:00 to 2:30 AM.

The route ascends steady 30-degree snow slopes across the central glacier bowl. The air is bone-chillingly cold and thin, demanding steady breathing, rhythmic steps, and unyielding mental focus. As the eastern horizon transitions from deep indigo to blazing gold, the first sunbeams illuminate the towering crown of Kanchenjunga.

The final 40-meter summit ridge steepens to approximately 40 to 45 degrees. Here, our Sherpas fix a safety rope. Clipping your jumar onto the line, you make the final exhilarating push onto the summit dome of Mera Peak Central (6,476 m / 21,247 ft).

Standing at the pinnacle of Nepal's highest trekking peak, you are surrounded by an unmatched 360-degree amphitheater of five 8,000-meter giants: Mount Everest (8,848 m), Lhotse (8,516 m), Makalu (8,481 m), Cho Oyu (8,188 m), and Kanchenjunga (8,586 m), along with Baruntse, Chamlang, and Ama Dablam. After celebratory photos and summit embrace, we carefully abseil and retrace our steps down to High Camp for hot soup, then continue descending to the comfort of Khare for a triumphant celebration and deeply earned sleep.

Day 15: Contingency / Weather Reserve Day in Khare (5,045m)

Khare – 5,045 m / 16,551 ft

Reserve day for summit safety
Accommodation: High-Altitude Teahouse Lodge
Activity: Built-in weather & acclimatization buffer
Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner
Overnight: Khare (5,045 m)

Himalayan weather can be unpredictable. High winds, heavy snowfall, or individual acclimatization delays can occasionally disrupt a summit schedule. Having this dedicated Day 15 contingency day built directly into the itinerary is a key reason for our outstanding summit success rate.

If the summit push was postponed on Day 14 due to adverse conditions, we launch our second summit bid today with renewed energy and favorable weather windows.

If the summit was successfully achieved on Day 14 as scheduled, this day becomes a glorious, pressure-free rest day in Khare. Relax by the teahouse stove, share stories and photos with fellow climbers, take short walks around the moraine, or enjoy an extra leisurely descent pace down the valley.

With the summit triumph behind you, we retrace our steps down the Hinku Valley. Moving downhill with increasing atmospheric oxygen makes walking feel effortless and invigorating compared to the arduous ascent.

We hike past the Dig Glacier moraines and Thangnak, pausing for tea and a hot lunch while looking back at the colossal rock wall of Charpate and Kyashar. Continuing south along the Hinku Khola river banks, the alpine scrub gradually gives way to birch, juniper, and fragrant pine forests.

We arrive back at Kothe (3,600 m). The thick, oxygen-rich air at 3,600 meters feels like a luxury. Tonight, celebrate with your Sherpa guides and porters over a festive dinner and local Chhyang (Himalayan rice wine) or Tongba.

Today presents an exciting, rewarding finale to our mountain trek. Rather than retracing the long southern loop via Pangkongma, our expedition tackles the direct alpine pass of Zatrwa La (4,610 m).

Setting off early from Kothe, the trail climbs steeply westward out of the Hinku Valley through dense rhododendron and birch forests to the high pasture of Thuli Kharka (4,300 m). From here, a rocky traverse leads to the crest of the Zatrwa La Pass. Standing at the pass, decorated with prayer flags, enjoy a spectacular final panorama looking across both the Hinku and Dudh Koshi river systems.

Crossing the pass, we make a steady descent toward the Khumbu Valley. Through fragrant pine groves and welcoming mani walls, the lights of Lukla come into view. We check into a comfortable lodge in Lukla, enjoy hot showers, and host a farewell banquet honoring our heroic Sherpas and porters.

Double Boots vs Trekking Boots

Regular hiking boots are perfect for the approach up to Khare (5,045 m). Above Khare, insulated 6000m double mountaineering boots (e.g., La Sportiva G2 SM or Scarpa Phantom 6000) are strictly required to protect against frostbite during the -25°C summit push.

Summit Night Cold Protection

Summit departure is at 2:00 AM in pitch blackness and piercing windchill. Layer an 800-fill down jacket over heavy merino base layers, wear insulated mitts with liner gloves, use chemical toe warmers, and keep spare lithium headlamp batteries inside your inner coat pocket.

Accommodation during the 18-day Mera Peak expedition transitions through three distinct comfort zones: standard city hospitality in Kathmandu, rustic family-run teahouses across the Solukhumbu and Hinku valleys, and specialized alpine camping at Mera High Camp (5,780 m).

Kathmandu Hotel Comfort

In Kathmandu, you stay 3 nights at a 3-star boutique hotel in the quiet outskirts of Thamel. Rooms include en-suite bathrooms with reliable 24-hour hot water, comfortable mattresses, air conditioning, high-speed Wi-Fi, and daily buffet breakfast. This provides an ideal staging base for pre-expedition gear checks and a welcoming sanctuary after your mountain summit.

Hinku Valley Mountain Teahouses

During the trek from Paiya to Khare, accommodation is in local Sherpa- and Rai-run teahouses. Rooms are simple, unheated twin-share bedrooms with wooden beds, foam mattresses, pillows, and blankets. Because the Hinku Valley is significantly less commercialized than the Everest Base Camp highway, teahouses here retain an authentic, rustic alpine charm. In settlements like Thangnak and Khare, dining halls are warmed in the evening by a central cast-iron stove fueled by dried yak dung or firewood, creating a lively social hub for hot tea and hearty meals.

High-Altitude Expedition Tents at High Camp (5,780 m)

At Mera High Camp (5,780 m), where no permanent buildings exist, our Sherpa team sets up a specialized tented campsite. We provide rugged 4-season geodesic dome tents (Mountain Hardwear Trango 3 or The North Face VE 25) designed to withstand gale-force Himalayan winds and heavy snowfall. Inside, each climber is equipped with a thick closed-cell insulating foam sleeping pad. We strongly advise using an expedition sleeping bag rated to at least -20°C (-4°F) to ensure warm, restorative rest before the midnight summit push.

Available Food & Mountain Nutrition: Dal Bhat, High-Calorie Meals & Garlic Soup

Proper high-altitude nutrition is critical for sustaining energy, staying warm, and recovering muscular strength during an 18-day climbing expedition where your basal metabolic rate can increase by 30% to 50%.

The Power of Traditional Dal Bhat

Throughout the mountain lodges, the undisputed staple is Dal Bhat Tarkari—steamed rice, savory lentil soup, spiced vegetable curry, and fresh spinach. Highly nutritious, carbohydrate-dense, easily digestible at high elevation, and always served with generous free second helpings, it remains the fuel of choice for Sherpa mountaineers.

Diverse Lodge Menus

Teahouse menus in Paiya, Kothe, Thangnak, and Khare also feature hearty Western and Sherpa dishes:

  • Breakfast: Oatmeal porridge, muesli with warm milk, pancakes, Tibetan bread with honey/jam, scrambled or fried eggs, and hash browns.
  • Lunch & Dinner: Sherpa stew (Syakpa), fried noodles (Chowmein), momos (steamed dumplings), vegetable fried rice, macaroni pasta with yak cheese, and potato rostis.
  • Altitude Soups: Fresh garlic soup is enthusiastically recommended by Sherpa guides due to its proven vasodilating properties that aid oxygen circulation in the blood.

High Camp Dining & Summit Nutrition

At Mera High Camp, our expedition cooks prepare hot, high-carbohydrate meals such as vegetable pasta, instant porridge, and hot soups. For the 2:00 AM summit push, carry lightweight, high-energy snacks that won't freeze solid (honey stinger waffles, chocolate bars, energy gels, mixed dried fruits, and nuts) tucked inside your down jacket pockets close to body heat.

Drinking Water & Hydration Safety (4–5 Liters Daily)

Staying aggressively hydrated is the single most effective physiological defense against Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS). In the dry, thin air above 4,000 meters, you lose significant moisture through rapid respiratory rate alone. We mandate drinking 4 to 5 liters of fluids daily.

Safe Drinking Water Protocols

Never drink untreated tap or stream water in Nepal. We recommend two safe, eco-friendly strategies:

  • Chemical Purification: Fill wide-mouth Nalgene bottles with lodge tap water and treat with chlorine dioxide tablets (Aquatabs / Katadyn Micropur) or iodine. Allow the full 30-minute reaction time before drinking.
  • Boiled Lodge Water: Teahouses sell boiled drinking water by the liter or thermos. Boiling kills 100% of waterborne pathogens and provides comforting warmth when poured into your bottle at night (doubling as a bed warmer inside your sleeping bag!).

Freeze Prevention on Summit Night

On summit morning, temperatures plummet to -20°C. Standard water bladder hoses freeze solid within 15 minutes and should be avoided. Instead, carry a 1-liter vacuum-insulated thermos flask filled with hot lemon tea, plus a 1-liter wide-mouth Nalgene bottle stored inside an insulated thermal sleeve inside your backpack.

Expedition Difficulty & Mountaineering Classification (Alpine PD)

Mera Peak Central (6,476 m / 21,247 ft) is classified as Alpine PD (Peu Difficile / Slightly Difficult) on the French adjectival mountaineering scale. While technically straightforward compared to peaks like Island Peak or Ama Dablam, it is physically demanding due to extreme altitude, freezing temperatures, and long sustained walking hours.

What Makes Mera Peak Physically Challenging?

  • Extreme Altitude: At nearly 6,500 meters, each breath delivers under 45% of sea-level oxygen. Simple movements demand heavy physical exertion.
  • Sustained Summit Day: The summit push from High Camp to the top and down to Khare involves 9 to 11 hours of continuous movement across 1,400 meters of elevation change.
  • Freezing Temperatures: Wind chills at High Camp and the summit ridge regularly drop to between -15°C and -25°C.

Technical Requirements

The glacier ascent consists mostly of low-angle snow walking (20° to 30° gradients) roped together in teams of 3 to 4. The only steep technical section is the final 40-meter summit headwall, which steepens to 40°–45°. Our Climbing Sherpas fix a dynamic safety line here, allowing you to ascend safely using a mechanical jumar ascender and abseil down with a figure-8 descender. All necessary technical skills are taught and practiced thoroughly on Day 12 at Khare Base Camp.

Best Time for Mera Peak Climbing: Spring vs Autumn Windows

Climbing Mera Peak is optimal during Nepal's two primary mountaineering seasons: Spring (late March to May) and Autumn (late September to November). Each season offers unique weather characteristics and mountain conditions.

Autumn Season (Late September to November) — The Peak Season

Autumn is widely considered the premier climbing window for Mera Peak:

  • Sky Clarity: The monsoon washes away atmospheric dust, leaving razor-sharp crystal skies and virtually guaranteed panoramas of the 5 eight-thousanders.
  • Stable Weather: High-pressure systems bring settled, predictable weather patterns with low wind speeds on the summit ridge.
  • Colder Nights: Temperatures are crisp and cold, dipping to -20°C at High Camp by November.

Spring Season (April to May) — Warmer Temperatures & Blooming Flora

Spring is favored by climbers who prefer milder temperatures and vibrant nature:

  • Rhododendron Blooms: The forests of Paiya and Pangkongma burst into brilliant scarlet, pink, and white blossoms.
  • Warmer Days: Daytime temperatures are warmer, making high camps and summit mornings less punishing.
  • Afternoon Clouds: Pre-monsoon thermal convection can build afternoon clouds and occasional snow flurries, making dawn summit pushes essential.

Acclimatization Strategy & Altitude Sickness Prevention (Khare & High Camp)

Ascending safely to 6,476 m requires methodical physiological adaptation. Our 18-day itinerary is purposefully structured according to established high-altitude medicine guidelines to virtually eliminate the risk of severe altitude illness.

Our Golden Acclimatization Safeguards:

  • The Southern Approach via Paiya & Pangkongma: Unlike rushed routes that force trekkers over the 4,600m Zatrwa La on Day 2, our route approaches south through Paiya (2,730 m), allowing 5 full days of gentle elevation gain below 3,600 meters.
  • Active Rest Day in Thangnak (Day 10): We hike up to Sabai Tsho Lake and Charpate Ridge (5,000 m) before descending to sleep at Thangnak (4,356 m), adhering strictly to the principle: Climb high, sleep low.
  • Two Nights at Khare Base Camp (5,045 m): Spending Days 11 and 12 at Khare stabilizes arterial oxygen saturation before ascending to High Camp.
  • Daily Pulse Oximetry Logs: Your lead guide checks your resting heart rate and blood oxygen percentage (SpO2) every morning and evening.
  • Diamox (Acetazolamide): Climbers may consider taking prophylactic low-dose Diamox (125mg twice daily) starting around Kothe, following consultation with their travel physician.

Pre-Climbing Alpine Training at Khare Base Camp (Glacier Clinic)

You do not need to arrive in Nepal as an experienced alpinist. Day 12 at Khare Base Camp (5,045 m) is an intensive, practical glacier clinic conducted on real glacial ice and moraine slopes by our UIAGM/NMA-certified Climbing Sherpas.

Skills Covered During the Khare Clinic:

  • Crampon Fitting & Footwork: Learning flat-footing (French technique) and front-pointing on hard alpine ice to maximize traction and avoid snagging pant cuffs.
  • Ice Axe Technique: Proper walking grip (piolet-canne), self-belay, and self-arrest practice to immediately arrest a simulated fall on snow slopes.
  • Ascending Fixed Lines (Jumar): Clipping and advancing a mechanical ascender along fixed static ropes, managing safety slings at intermediate anchor points.
  • Abseiling / Rappelling: Controlled descent using figure-8 descenders or ATC belay devices with backup friction knots.
  • Rope Team Management: Distance spacing, walking in unison, and maintaining proper tension on tied-in glaciated rope teams.

This session builds immense confidence and guarantees that every team member operates with muscle memory and calm precision on summit day.

Summit Day Breakdown: 2:00 AM Alpine Start, Crampons & Fixed Ropes

Summit Day on Mera Peak Central (6,476 m) is an extraordinary, unforgettable alpine challenge requiring mental grit, steady pacing, and endurance.

Chronological Summit Push Schedule:

  • 01:30 AM — Wake-up Call: Hot tea, porridge, and biscuits served in your tent. Suit up in full thermal down layers, double boots, harnesses, and headlamps.
  • 02:00 AM — Departure: Rope up with your Climbing Sherpa (1:2 ratio) and step onto the crisp, frozen snowfield under starry Himalayan skies.
  • 02:00 AM to 05:30 AM — Glacier Bowl Ascent: A steady, rhythmic zigzag ascent up 25°–30° snow slopes across the open glacier bowl. Air is crisp, calm, and freezing (-15°C to -25°C).
  • 05:45 AM — Golden Sunrise: Dawn breaks over the eastern horizon, setting Kanchenjunga and Makalu ablaze with golden and magenta hues.
  • 07:30 AM — The Summit Ridge: Reach the base of the final summit pyramid. Clip onto the 40-meter fixed safety rope installed by our Sherpas.
  • 08:30 AM — SUMMIT APEX (6,476 m): Step onto the highest trekking peak in Nepal. Celebrate, take photos, and absorb the breathtaking 360-degree panorama of Everest, Lhotse, Makalu, Cho Oyu, and Kanchenjunga!
  • 09:30 AM to 12:30 PM — Safe Descent: Controlled abseil down the ridge and retrace rope tracks down to High Camp for hot soup, then continue descending safely to Khare for celebration.

High Camp Logistics (5,780m): Tents, Sub-Zero Sleep & Sunset Views

Perched dramatically on a rocky outcrop behind the Mera Glacier edge, Mera High Camp (5,780 m / 18,963 ft) is one of the highest sleeping locations in the world. Despite its stark, frozen environment, proper logistics turn it into a secure, memorable haven.

Expedition Infrastructure:

Rest is the primary focus. Upon arriving in the afternoon, remove crampons, slip into dry socks, and climb inside your sleeping bag to conserve body warmth. Mountain Weather, Temperature Ranges & Windchill at 6,476m

High mountain weather in the Makalu-Barun and Solukhumbu ranges changes rapidly with altitude. Being equipped for extreme cold and variable conditions is the key to comfort and safety.

Seasonal Temperature Breakdown:

  • Lower Valleys (Paiya / Kothe, 2,700m–3,600m): Daytime: 12°C to 18°C (comfortable trekking in light softshell). Nighttime: 2°C to 7°C.
  • Khare Base Camp (5,045m): Daytime: 4°C to 8°C in direct sun. Nighttime: -5°C to -10°C.
  • Mera High Camp (5,780m): Daytime: 0°C to -4°C. Nighttime: -12°C to -18°C.
  • Mera Summit Ridge (6,476m): Early morning summit temperatures range from -15°C to -25°C. With wind chill factored in, perceived temperatures can drop to -30°C.

Our expedition leaders monitor daily satellite weather forecasts via satellite phone to ensure summit attempts coincide with clear skies and wind speeds below 30 km/h.

Cultural Etiquette: Rai & Sherpa Traditions in the Hinku Valley

The southern approach through Paiya, Pangkongma, and the Hinku Valley passes through traditional lands inhabited by Rai, Chhetri, and Sherpa communities. Observing local customs enriches your journey and demonstrates heartfelt respect.

Key Cultural Guidelines:

  • Mani Walls & Chortens: Always pass Buddhist mani walls, chortens, and stupas on your right side (clockwise direction). Never sit on or step over carved sacred stones.
  • Lungsumgba Gompa Blessing: In Thangnak, our climbing team stops at the sacred rock-carved shrine of Lungsumgba. Lighting a butter lamp and receiving a blessing from the resident lama is an auspicious Buddhist tradition before climbing.
  • Greetings: Greet locals with palms pressed together at chest level saying "Namaste" (or "Tashi Delek" in Sherpa communities).
  • Photography: Always politely ask permission before photographing local residents, monks, or the interior of sacred shrines.

Frequently Asked Questions for Mera Peak Climbing Trek

General & Route Information

What is the exact elevation of Mera Peak and which summit is climbed?

Mera Peak has three distinct summits: Mera Central (6,476 m / 21,247 ft), Mera North (6,461 m), and Mera South (6,065 m). Our expedition targets Mera Peak Central, which is the highest officially designated trekking peak in Nepal and provides the legendary 360-degree panoramic vantage point overlooking five 8,000-meter giants.

How does Mera Peak compare in difficulty to Island Peak and Lobuche East?

Mera Peak (6,476 m) is significantly higher than Island Peak (6,189 m) and Lobuche East (6,119 m), but technically less steep. While Island Peak features an exposed knife-edge ridge and steep headwall, Mera Peak is predominantly a non-technical glacier climb (Alpine Grade PD) with only the final 40-meter summit dome requiring fixed lines. It demands greater cardiovascular endurance and cold tolerance due to its higher altitude.

Which 8,000-meter peaks can be seen from the summit?

From the central summit dome, you enjoy an unobstructed 360-degree view of five of the world's six highest mountains: Mount Everest (8,848 m), Kanchenjunga (8,586 m), Lhotse (8,516 m), Makalu (8,481 m), and Cho Oyu (8,188 m), plus dramatic views of Chamlang, Baruntse, and Ama Dablam.

Why does your itinerary approach via Paiya and Pangkongma instead of the direct Zatrwa La route?

Crossing the 4,610-meter Zatrwa La Pass on Day 2 directly from Lukla is notorious for causing severe Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) and trek abandonment. Our southern approach through Paiya (2,730 m) and Pangkongma (2,846 m) provides 5 full days of gradual physiological adaptation below 3,600m, yielding an industry-leading summit success rate over 92%.

What happens on the built-in Day 15 weather contingency day?

If high summit winds or snow flurries delay our Day 14 push, Day 15 is used immediately for our second summit window. If the summit was already achieved on Day 14, Day 15 serves as a leisurely rest day in Khare or allows an extra slow, enjoyable descent down the Hinku Valley.

What permits are legally required to climb Mera Peak?

Three official permits are mandatory: (1) Nepal Mountaineering Association (NMA) Mera Peak climbing permit, (2) Makalu-Barun National Park entry permit, and (3) Khumbu Pasang Lhamu Rural Municipality entry fee. All fees, documentation, and the NMA environmental garbage deposit are fully included in our package.

Do I need double mountaineering boots, or are standard trekking boots enough?

Standard trekking boots are suitable up to Khare Base Camp (5,045 m). Above Khare, insulated 6000m double mountaineering boots (plastic or hybrid double boots like La Sportiva G2 SM or Scarpa Phantom 6000) are non-negotiable to prevent severe frostbite during the -25°C summit morning.

Can I rent all technical mountaineering gear in Nepal?

Yes. Climbing boots, crampons, ice axes, harnesses, helmets, and jumar ascenders can be rented either in Kathmandu during Day 2 or directly at Khare Base Camp. Renting in Khare is popular because it eliminates carrying heavy boots over the lower forest trail.

What type of crampons and ice axe do I need?

You need 12-point steel crampons with anti-balling plates (semi-automatic or automatic step-in to match your double boots) and a classic straight-shaft walking ice axe (60 cm to 70 cm). Technical ice climbing tools are not required.

What sleeping bag rating is necessary for High Camp?

A four-season down sleeping bag with a comfort rating of -20°C to -25°C (-4°F to -13°F) is essential for Mera High Camp (5,780 m). High-quality down bags can be rented in Kathmandu for ~$2 USD per day.

What is the accommodation like in the Hinku Valley teahouses?

Teahouses are clean, family-run mountain lodges offering twin-share bedrooms with foam mattresses, pillows, and blankets. Common dining areas feature a central wood/yak-dung stove. Bathrooms are shared, typically Western-style or squat toilets.

How do we sleep at Mera High Camp (5,780 m)?

We sleep in premium 4-season geodesic expedition tents (Mountain Hardwear / North Face) erected by our Sherpa staff. Inside, thick insulating foam pads are laid out beneath your sub-zero sleeping bag. Hot meals and tea are served directly to your tent.

What kind of food is served on the trek and at High Camp?

Lodge dining rooms serve freshly prepared meals including traditional Dal Bhat, fried rice, noodle chowmein, momos, porridge, eggs, potato dishes, and Sherpa garlic stew. At High Camp, our expedition cooks prepare hot, high-energy pasta and vegetable stews.

Are hot showers and charging facilities available?

Hot gas or bucket showers are available up to Khare (costing approx. $4–$6 USD). Solar/hydro electricity is available in dining rooms to charge phones and cameras for a modest fee ($2–$4 USD). High Camp has no showers or electrical outlets.

How is drinking water handled above 5,000 meters?

In lodges, boiled water is purchased and treated with purification tablets. At High Camp, our Sherpas melt snow and boil water thoroughly for cooking, hot drinks, and filling insulated thermoses for the summit climb.

What is your climbing guide-to-client ratio on summit day?

We maintain a strict 1:2 ratio (one certified Climbing Sherpa for every two climbers) on summit day, with 1:1 available upon request. This ensures individual roping, personal pace monitoring, and immediate assistance at all times.

How do you monitor climber acclimatization and health?

Our lead guide conducts twice-daily health checks using pulse oximeters to record blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) and heart rate. We also evaluate the Lake Louise Score to catch early symptoms of altitude illness.

Should I take Diamox (Acetazolamide)?

Many climbers choose to take Diamox (125 mg to 250 mg twice daily) starting around Kothe (3,600 m) to stimulate breathing and speed acclimatization. Consult your personal physician before your trip.

What happens if someone in the team cannot continue or suffers from AMS?

Because we operate with multiple Sherpas and assistant guides, an unwell climber is safely escorted down to lower elevation immediately by a dedicated Sherpa, while the rest of the team continues safely toward the summit.

What level of mountaineering insurance is required?

You must hold a specialized policy covering alpine mountaineering and helicopter evacuation up to 6,500 meters (21,325 ft). Policies from Global Rescue, Ripcord, or World Nomads Explorer are commonly used.

What is the summit bonus for Climbing Sherpas and tipping guidelines?

A customary summit bonus of $150 to $250 USD per climber is awarded to your personal Climbing Sherpa upon reaching the summit. For trekking guides and porters, a communal tip pool of $120–$180 USD per climber is customary.

What ethnic communities will we encounter in the lower Hinku Valley?

The southern approach traverses traditional Rai and Chhetri farming hamlets before reaching Sherpa communities around Pangkongma. You will experience traditional animist and Buddhist cultural practices, terraced farms, and historic stone gompas.

What are your porter welfare standards?

We are strict adherents of the International Porter Protection Group (IPPG) guidelines: maximum 20 kg loads, fair living wages, warm protective clothing, insulated footwear, and full emergency medical and helicopter evacuation insurance.

How is expedition waste managed under NMA environmental regulations?
